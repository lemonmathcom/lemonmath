[[P1·d0a8]]
"Which expression is equivalent to…?" is one of the most common question stems in the Advanced Math domain, which has about 13 to 15 questions in the test. The good news is that these questions test only algebra that Vietnamese students learn in grades 8 and 9, and quick students solve them in under a minute. You also have two tools from the previous lessons to check your answer: [plugging in a number](lesson:sat-desmos-strategies) and graphing both expressions in Desmos.
[[P3·b6cf]]
Two polynomials are equivalent (equal for every $x$) exactly when their coefficients of $x^2$, of $x$ and their constants are equal. Why? If two polynomials are always equal, their difference is zero everywhere, and that only happens when every coefficient of the difference is zero. So one equation in the unknown turns into several simple equations for the coefficients.
[[P4·5f42]]
To simplify a fraction of polynomials, factor the top and the bottom and cancel common factors, keeping an eye on the values that make a denominator zero. To add fractions, use a common denominator, just as you do with ordinary fractions.
[[P5·f181]]
Rearranging a formula works just like solving an equation: step by step we bring the letter we want to one side, and leave the other letters as they are.
[[P6·08e6]]
- **Adding exponents when multiplying bases that differ.** $2^3\cdot3^2$ is $8\cdot9=72$, not $6^5$.
- **Squaring a sum term by term.** $(x+3)^2=x^2+6x+9$, not $x^2+9$.
- **Cancelling terms instead of factors.** In $\dfrac{x+6}{x+2}$ nothing cancels; in $\dfrac{x^2-9}{x+3}=\dfrac{(x-3)(x+3)}{x+3}$ the factor $x+3$ cancels.
- **Negative exponents.** $x^{-2}=\dfrac{1}{x^2}$, not $-x^2$.

SAT questions often put exactly these wrong results among the options, so do not worry if you have made one of these slips. When in doubt, plug in a number to check.
[[P7·f262]]
Type the original expression as a `y=` line and each option as another `y=` line. The equivalent option is the one whose graph lies exactly on top of the original. If the expressions have two letters, Desmos cannot draw them so easily, so plug in numbers for both letters instead.

[[P1·0537]]
Unit 5 is one of the most heavily weighted units of AB, and its ideas return in almost every free-response question that shows a graph or a table of $f'$. The calculations are usually short. What the exam really tests is reasoning: "Because $f'$ changes from negative to positive at $x=2$" earns a point; "because $f'(2)=0$" does not. So we train both the mathematics and the sentences. Much of the mathematics will be familiar from curve sketching at school; what is new is that AP wants the reasons in words.
[[P2·885b]]
$f$ is increasing where $f'>0$ and decreasing where $f'<0$, because the derivative is the slope of the graph. The places that matter most are where the function might turn round. A **critical point** is an interior $x$ where $f'(x)=0$ or $f'(x)$ does not exist.
[[P4·c68b]]
On a closed interval, the absolute maximum can only be in one of two kinds of place: at a critical point or at an endpoint. So we evaluate $f$ at every critical point and at both endpoints and compare: the largest value is the absolute maximum, the smallest the absolute minimum.
[[P6·c73c]]
For an optimization problem, follow a fixed routine: write the quantity to optimize as a function of one variable, state the domain, find the critical points, and justify the maximum or minimum with the candidates test, a sign chart or the second derivative. Stating the domain is the step people skip, yet it is what tells you which endpoints to compare.
[[P7·0049]]
- **"$f'(c)=0$, so there is an extremum."** Not enough: $f'$ must change sign (or use $f''$).
- **"$f''(c)=0$, so there is an inflection point."** Not enough: $f''$ must change sign.
- **Forgetting the endpoints** in the candidates test.
- **Reading the graph of $f'$ as if it were $f$.** On a graph of $f'$, extrema of $f$ are at the $x$-intercepts where the sign changes; inflection points of $f$ are at the peaks and valleys of $f'$.
- **Dropping the MVT conditions.** On the free-response section, state "continuous on $[a,b]$ and differentiable on $(a,b)$".

Do not worry if you have fallen into some of these traps; many students do. Before you conclude, ask yourself: does the derivative really change sign?

We have $2<3$. Add $4$ to both sides and we get $6<7$. Add $-5$ and we get $-3<-2$. The order stays the same. Why? Adding the same number to both sides slides both points the same distance along the number line: whichever point was on the left is still on the left.
[[P8·44a7]]
Again we have $2<3$. Multiplying both sides by $4$ gives $8<12$: the direction stays the same. But multiplying both sides by $-1$ gives $-2$ and $-3$, and $-2>-3$: the direction is **reversed**.
