A hypothetical journey is $37.48$ km long. For a quick plan, we may say about $37.5$ km or about $40$ km, depending on the accuracy needed. Both are useful but answer different questions. How do we choose a rounding place and judge whether a calculation is reasonable?

## rounding numbers and estimating results

**A short exploration.**
$37.48$ lies between $37.4$ and $37.5$ on the number line. It is closer to $37.5$ because the distance is $0.02$ rather than $0.08$. Therefore, to one decimal place, it rounds to $37.5$.

:::definition Rounding a number
To round to a place, inspect the digit immediately to its right. If that digit is less than $5$, keep the rounding digit; if it is at least $5$, increase it by $1$. Remove later decimal digits or replace later whole-number digits with $0$.
:::

:::definition Estimation
Estimation replaces numbers by nearby, easy values to predict the size of a result. Round numbers to a suitable common place or use compatible numbers, and state that the result is approximate.
:::

**Why does this work?**
Numbers on opposite sides of a midpoint have different distances to the two possible rounded values. Inspecting the next digit chooses the nearer value; a next digit of $5$ uses the convention of rounding up. Estimation does not replace an exact result, but provides a checking range: if an exact calculation is far from the estimate, check the sign, decimal point or operation.

**How to check your own work.** For a question on this topic, first state in words what is given and what must be found. Next write the rule you will use, substitute values or reason one step at a time, and then check the result in a different way: put it back into the data, estimate its size, or compare it with a diagram or number line. If two methods disagree, locate the first step where they differ instead of changing only the final answer. This habit catches sign errors, missing units and overlooked cases.

**Systems view.** rounding numbers and estimating results is not an isolated trick. Follow the chain: the data determine a model, the model determines the calculation, and the result must return to the original situation. When one piece of data changes, predict whether the result should increase, decrease or stay unchanged before calculating again.

## Worked examples and applications

:::example Rounding a decimal
Round $18.746$ to two decimal places.

**Solution.**
The hundredths digit is $4$; the next digit is $6\ge5$, so increase $4$ to $5$.

Therefore, $18.75$.
:::

:::example Rounding a natural number
Round $48\,372$ to the nearest thousand.

**Solution.**
The hundreds digit is $3<5$, so keep the thousands digit $8$ and replace later digits with $0$.

Therefore, $48\,000$.
:::

:::example Estimating a product
Estimate $19.8\cdot5.1$.

**Solution.**
Round $19.8\approx20$ and $5.1\approx5$, so $19.8\cdot5.1\approx20\cdot5=100$.

Therefore, about $100$.
:::

:::example Planning a journey
A route has three sections of $12.7$ km, $8.46$ km and $16.2$ km. Estimate the total to the nearest kilometre.

**Solution.**

*Understand.* Estimate the total; hundredths of a kilometre are unnecessary.

*Plan.* Round each section to the nearest kilometre and add.

*Solve.* Round each section: $12.7\approx13$, $8.46\approx8$ and $16.2\approx16$. The estimated total is $13+8+16=37$ km.

*Look back.* The exact total is $37.36$ km, close to $37$ km. The difference of $0.36$ km is consistent with the rounding level.

Therefore, the total is about $37$ km.
:::

## Common mistakes and connections

:::warning What can go wrong?
- **Inspecting the wrong digit.** Rounding $6.284$ to one decimal place by inspecting the digit $4$. The deciding digit is the hundredths digit $8$. $6.284\approx6.3$.
- **Using an equals sign for an estimate.** $49.2+20.7=70$. $70$ is only an approximate value. Write $49.2+20.7\approx70$.
:::

**Connections.** The rounding level must suit the decision being made. Measuring a desk may require centimetres, while planning a long journey may need only kilometres. Rounding too early in a chain of calculations can accumulate error, so keep extra digits in intermediate work and round only the final result when greater accuracy is needed. Estimate before performing the exact calculation so that the computed answer does not influence the prediction. For addition, round each term and add; for multiplication and division, choose compatible numbers that are easy to calculate mentally. Compare the sign, order of magnitude and unit of the result with the situation. This skill is essential when checking bills, travel time, measurements and calculator output. It also helps distinguish a sensible small difference from a decimal-point error that changes a quantity by a factor of $10$ or $100$.

## Summary

- Choose the rounding place to suit the required accuracy.
- Inspect the next digit: below $5$ round down; at least $5$ increase it by $1$.
- Use $\approx$ for an approximate value.
- Estimate first to check the size of an answer.
- Round only at the end when accumulated error matters.
