One bottle is filled to $3$ of $4$ equal parts; an identical bottle is divided into $8$ parts and filled to $6$ parts. Do $\dfrac34$ and $\dfrac68$ describe the same amount? We need an exact test for fractions written differently but having the same value.

## equivalent fractions and the fundamental property of fractions

**A short exploration.**
Split each quarter into $2$ equal parts. Three quarters become six eighths without changing the shaded amount: $\dfrac34=\dfrac{3\cdot2}{4\cdot2}=\dfrac68$. The unit parts change, but the part of the whole does not.

:::definition Fractions and equivalent fractions
For $a,b\in\mathbb Z$ with $b\ne0$, $\dfrac ab$ is a fraction. The fractions $\dfrac ab$ and $\dfrac cd$ are equivalent when $ad=bc$.
:::

:::definition Fundamental property of fractions
Multiplying numerator and denominator by the same integer other than $0$ does not change the value: $\dfrac ab=\dfrac{am}{bm}$ ($m\ne0$). Dividing both by the same common factor other than $0$ also preserves the value.
:::

**Why does this work?**
In $\dfrac ab=\dfrac{am}{bm}$, multiplying numerator and denominator by $m$ is multiplying the fraction by $\dfrac mm=1$. The value stays unchanged because it is multiplied by $1$. Cross-products follow: if $\dfrac ab=\dfrac cd$, multiplying both sides by $bd\ne0$ gives $ad=bc$; reversing the steps proves the converse.

**How to check your own work.** For a question on this topic, first state in words what is given and what must be found. Next write the rule you will use, substitute values or reason one step at a time, and then check the result in a different way: put it back into the data, estimate its size, or compare it with a diagram or number line. If two methods disagree, locate the first step where they differ instead of changing only the final answer. This habit catches sign errors, missing units and overlooked cases.

**Systems view.** equivalent fractions and the fundamental property of fractions is not an isolated trick. Follow the chain: the data determine a model, the model determines the calculation, and the result must return to the original situation. When one piece of data changes, predict whether the result should increase, decrease or stay unchanged before calculating again.

## Worked examples and applications

:::example Extending a fraction
Write $\dfrac{-5}{7}$ as a fraction with denominator $35$.

**Solution.**
Since $35=7\cdot5$, multiply numerator and denominator by $5$: $\dfrac{-5}{7}=\dfrac{-25}{35}$.

Therefore, $\dfrac{-25}{35}$.
:::

:::example Testing two fractions
Are $\dfrac{14}{21}$ and $\dfrac{10}{15}$ equivalent?

**Solution.**
$14\cdot15=210$ and $21\cdot10=210$. The cross-products are equal, so the fractions are equivalent.

Therefore, $\dfrac{14}{21}=\dfrac{10}{15}$.
:::

:::example Writing in simplest form
Write $\dfrac{-84}{126}$ in its simplest form.

**Solution.**
$\operatorname{HCF}(84,126)=42$, so $\dfrac{-84}{126}=\dfrac{-84\div42}{126\div42}=\dfrac{-2}{3}$.

Therefore, $-\dfrac23$.
:::

:::example Two ways to cut a cake
A cake has $9$ of its $12$ equal pieces eaten. Is that amount equal to $\dfrac34$ of the cake?

**Solution.**

*Understand.* The same cake is divided into different numbers of parts; compare the two fractions.

*Plan.* Use cross-products or simplify $\dfrac9{12}$.

*Solve.* Compare $\dfrac9{12}$ and $\dfrac34$: $9\cdot4=36$ and $12\cdot3=36$, so the fractions are equivalent.

*Look back.* $\dfrac9{12}=\dfrac{9\div3}{12\div3}=\dfrac34$ gives a second check. What changes if $8$ of $12$ pieces are used?

Therefore, the amount is $\dfrac34$ of the cake.
:::

## Common mistakes and connections

:::warning What can go wrong?
- **Multiplying only the numerator.** Wrong: $\dfrac35=\dfrac65$. Changing only the numerator doubles the amount. Correct: multiply both by the same number, for example $\dfrac35=\dfrac6{10}$.
- **Allowing a denominator of $0$.** $\dfrac50$ is treated as a fraction. Division by $0$ is undefined. A denominator must always be different from $0$.
:::

**Connections.** Equivalent fractions underpin common denominators, comparison and fraction calculations. Simplest form also reveals divisibility in the numerator and denominator; later, the same idea of multiplying by $1$ appears in algebraic manipulation and in later calculations too.

## Summary

- A fraction denominator must be different from $0$.
- $\dfrac ab=\dfrac cd$ if and only if $ad=bc$.
- Multiplying or dividing numerator and denominator by the same number other than $0$ preserves the value.
- Simplify until numerator and denominator have no common factor greater than $1$.
- A negative sign may be placed in the numerator: $\dfrac{-a}{b}=-\dfrac ab$.
