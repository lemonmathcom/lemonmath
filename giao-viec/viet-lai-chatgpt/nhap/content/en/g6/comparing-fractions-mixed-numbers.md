Two groups complete $\dfrac56$ and $\dfrac78$ of a model. Looking only at $5<7$ is not enough because sixths and eighths have different sizes. Which group has completed more, and how can we compare fairly?

## comparing fractions and positive mixed numbers

**A short exploration.**
Write $\dfrac56$ and $\dfrac78$ over the common denominator $24$: $\dfrac56=\dfrac{20}{24}$ and $\dfrac78=\dfrac{21}{24}$. With equal-sized parts, the numerators show that $\dfrac56<\dfrac78$.

:::definition Comparing fractions
For fractions with the same positive denominator, the one with greater numerator is greater. For different denominators, write them over a common positive denominator and compare numerators; cross-products may also be used. Every positive fraction is greater than every negative fraction.
:::

:::definition Positive mixed numbers
A positive mixed number $a\dfrac bc$ has whole-number part $a$ and fractional part $\dfrac bc$, where $a\in\mathbb N$ and $0<b<c$. We have $a\dfrac bc=\dfrac{ac+b}{c}$.
:::

**Why does this work?**
A common denominator does not change a fraction; it only chooses the same unit part. With a positive common denominator, more parts means a greater value. For cross-products, $\dfrac ab<\dfrac cd$ with $b,d>0$ is equivalent to $ad<bc$ because multiplying by the positive number $bd$ preserves the inequality direction.

**How to check your own work.** For a question on this topic, first state in words what is given and what must be found. If two methods disagree, locate the first step where they differ instead of changing only the final answer. This habit catches sign errors, missing units and overlooked cases.

**Systems view.** comparing fractions and positive mixed numbers is not an isolated trick. Follow the chain: the data determine a model, the model determines the calculation, and the result must return to the original situation. When one piece of data changes, predict whether the result should increase, decrease or stay unchanged before calculating again.

## Worked examples and applications

:::example A common denominator
Compare $\dfrac{-7}{13}$ and $\dfrac{-4}{13}$.

**Solution.**
The denominators are equal and positive; since $-7<-4$, $\dfrac{-7}{13}<\dfrac{-4}{13}$.

Therefore, $\dfrac{-7}{13}<\dfrac{-4}{13}$.
:::

:::example Different denominators
Compare $\dfrac7{12}$ and $\dfrac5{8}$.

**Solution.**
Using denominator $24$, $\dfrac7{12}=\dfrac{14}{24}$ and $\dfrac58=\dfrac{15}{24}$, so $\dfrac7{12}<\dfrac58$.

Therefore, $\dfrac7{12}<\dfrac58$.
:::

:::example Converting a mixed number
Convert $3\dfrac25$ to a fraction and $\dfrac{29}{6}$ to a mixed number.

**Solution.**
$3\dfrac25=\dfrac{3\cdot5+2}{5}=\dfrac{17}{5}$. Since $29\div6=4$ remainder $5$, $\dfrac{29}{6}=4\dfrac56$.

Therefore, $\dfrac{17}{5}$ and $4\dfrac56$.
:::

:::example Progress of two groups
Group A completes $\dfrac56$ of a model and group B completes $\dfrac78$. Which group completes more?

**Solution.**

*Understand.* Compare two parts of the same whole.

*Plan.* Use the positive common denominator $24$.

*Solve.* $\dfrac56=\dfrac{20}{24}$ and $\dfrac78=\dfrac{21}{24}$; since $21>20$, group B completes more.

*Look back.* Both fractions are near $1$; $\dfrac78$ lacks $\dfrac18$ while $\dfrac56$ lacks $\dfrac16$. Since $\dfrac18<\dfrac16$, the conclusion is reasonable.

Therefore, group B completes more.
:::

## Common mistakes and connections

:::warning What can go wrong?
- **Comparing numerators with different denominators.** Wrong: $\dfrac35>\dfrac49$ because $4>3$. Fifths and ninths are different-sized parts. $3\cdot9=27>20=4\cdot5$, so $\dfrac35>\dfrac49$.
- **Ignoring a negative sign.** Wrong: $-\dfrac25>-\dfrac13$ because $\dfrac25>\dfrac13$. Taking opposites reverses the order. $-\dfrac25<-\dfrac13$.
:::

**Connections.** Comparing fractions uses two familiar ideas: choose the same unit and preserve order when multiplying by a positive number. Mixed numbers make measurements greater than $1$ easy to read; fractions greater than $1$ are usually easier for calculations and often reduce the risk of arithmetic errors.

## Summary

- With the same positive denominator, compare numerators.
- With different denominators, use a common denominator or cross-products.
- A negative number is less than $0$ and a positive number is greater than $0$.
- $a\dfrac bc=\dfrac{ac+b}{c}$.
- For positive mixed numbers, compare whole-number parts first, then fractional parts.
