A hypothetical class has $18$ students taking part in sport out of $30$. Saying $18$ alone hides the class size; the ratio $18:30=\dfrac35$ gives the share, while $60\%$ makes comparison with another class easy. How do we move between these forms and use them to find a part or a whole?

## ratios and percentages

**A short exploration.**
$18:30=\dfrac{18}{30}=\dfrac35=0.6=60\%$. The same relationship is written as a ratio, fraction, decimal and percentage. A percentage uses the standard denominator $100$.

:::definition Ratio
The ratio of $a$ to $b$ ($b\ne0$) is the quotient $a\div b=\dfrac ab$. Convert two quantities to the same unit before forming a ratio.
:::

:::definition Percentage
The percentage of $a$ relative to $b$ is $\dfrac ab\cdot100\%$. To find $p\%$ of $N$, calculate $N\cdot\dfrac p{100}$; to find the whole when part $A$ is $p\%$, calculate $A\div\dfrac p{100}$.
:::

**Why does this work?**
A percentage is a fraction with denominator $100$, so fraction part–whole methods apply directly. Converting to the same unit ensures that numerator and denominator measure the same kind of quantity; otherwise the ratio is not a meaningful comparison.

**How to check your own work.** For a question on this topic, first state in words what is given and what must be found. Next write the rule you will use, substitute values or reason one step at a time, and then check the result in a different way: put it back into the data, estimate its size, or compare it with a diagram or number line. If two methods disagree, locate the first step where they differ instead of changing only the final answer. This habit catches sign errors, missing units and overlooked cases.

**Systems view.** ratios and percentages is not an isolated trick. Follow the chain: the data determine a model, the model determines the calculation, and the result must return to the original situation. When one piece of data changes, predict whether the result should increase, decrease or stay unchanged before calculating again.

## Worked examples and applications

:::example Simplifying a ratio
Simplify the ratio $42:63$.

**Solution.**
$42:63=\dfrac{42}{63}=\dfrac23$, so the ratio in simplest form is $2:3$.

Therefore, $2:3$.
:::

:::example Converting units before a ratio
Find the ratio of $75$ cm to $1.5$ m.

**Solution.**
$1.5$ m $=150$ cm, so the ratio is $75:150=1:2$.

Therefore, $1:2$.
:::

:::example Finding a percentage
$18$ of $30$ students take part in sport. Find the percentage.

**Solution.**
$\dfrac{18}{30}\cdot100\%=60\%$.

Therefore, $60\%$.
:::

:::example A price after a discount
A hypothetical product costs $320\,000$ dong and is discounted by $15\%$. Find the discount and the sale price.

**Solution.**

*Understand.* The original price and discount percentage are known; find the discount and then the remainder.

*Plan.* Find $15\%$ of the price, then subtract it from the original.

*Solve.* The discount is $320\,000\cdot\dfrac{15}{100}=48\,000$ dong. The sale price is $320\,000-48\,000=272\,000$ dong.

*Look back.* The sale price $272\,000$ dong is $85\%$ of $320\,000$ dong, so the result is consistent.

Therefore, $48\,000$ dong off; sale price $272\,000$ dong.
:::

## Common mistakes and connections

:::warning What can go wrong?
- **Not converting to the same unit.** The ratio of $50$ cm to $2$ m is written as $50:2$. The quantities use different units. $2$ m $=200$ cm, so the ratio is $50:200=1:4$.
- **Confusing the discount with the remaining price.** A $20\%$ discount makes the new price $20\%$ of the old price. The remaining fraction is $100\%-20\%=80\%$. The new price is $80\%$ of the old price.
:::

**Connections.** A ratio describes a relative relationship, while a percentage uses the standard base $100$. The same absolute number can have very different meaning when the whole changes. When solving a problem, separate three layers: the data, the mathematical relationship and the conclusion. Before substituting values, state why the chosen rule applies; after each step, check signs, units and conditions. A diagram, small table or simple case often reveals a missing assumption. Estimate before calculating so that you know whether the answer should be large or small, positive or negative. After obtaining a result, use an independent check: substitute it back into the data, measure it on the diagram, or test a boundary case. If one item of data changes, predict which part of the method changes and which part remains valid. This approach turns a formula into a connected system of reasons and makes the idea usable in an unfamiliar situation instead of only in a memorised exercise. It also produces written work that another student can follow, question and verify line by line.

## Summary

- $a:b=\dfrac ab$ with $b\ne0$.
- Convert quantities to the same unit before forming a ratio.
- Percentage: $\dfrac ab\cdot100\%$.
- $p\%$ of $N$ is $N\cdot\dfrac p{100}$.
- Sale price equals original price minus the discount.
