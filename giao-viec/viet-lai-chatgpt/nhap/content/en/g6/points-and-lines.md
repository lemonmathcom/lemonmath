On a hypothetical straight route, stations $A,B,C$ occur in that order. Saying “$B$ lies between $A$ and $C$” gives more information than collinearity: it fixes their order. If a journey starts at $A$ and continues indefinitely through $B$, which mathematical object models it?

## points, lines, betweenness and rays

**A short exploration.**
Mark two distinct points $A,B$. Exactly one line passes through both. Place $C$ beyond $B$ on that line: $B$ lies between $A,C$. The part starting at $A$, passing through $B$ and continuing indefinitely is ray $AB$.

:::definition Points and lines
A point is named by a capital letter; a line is often named by a lower-case letter or two points on it. Exactly one line passes through two distinct points. Three or more points on one line are collinear.
:::

:::definition Betweenness and rays
If $A,B,C$ are collinear and $B$ lies on the part joining $A$ to $C$, then $B$ is between $A,C$. Ray $AB$ has endpoint $A$, passes through $B$ and continues indefinitely in that direction. Two rays with a common endpoint and opposite directions are opposite rays.
:::

**Why does this work?**
Two points determine both the position and direction of a line, so two different lines cannot pass through both. Ray names are ordered: the first letter is the endpoint, so rays $AB$ and $BA$ are usually different. Betweenness applies only to three collinear points.

## Worked examples and applications

:::example Reading notation
Line $d$ passes through $M,N$ but not $P$. Write the relationships in symbols.

**Solution.**
Write $M\in d$, $N\in d$ and $P\notin d$.

Therefore, $M,N\in d$; $P\notin d$.
:::

:::example Identifying the point between
Along a line, the order is $D,A,B,C$. Which point is between $A,C$?

**Solution.**
$B$ appears after $A$ and before $C$, so $B$ is between $A,C$.

Therefore, $B$.
:::

:::example Coincident rays
$A,B,C$ are collinear and $B$ lies between $A,C$. Compare rays $AB$ and $AC$.

**Solution.**
The rays have endpoint $A$ and the same direction from $A$ through $B,C$, so ray $AB$ coincides with ray $AC$.

Therefore, ray $AB$ coincides with ray $AC$.
:::

:::example A straight transport route
Stops occur in order $A,B,C,D$. A vehicle starts at $B$ and travels in the direction through $C$. Which listed stops lie on the journey ray?

**Solution.**

*Understand.* The point order gives the ray direction from $B$ through $C$.

*Plan.* Keep points from endpoint $B$ in the chosen direction and exclude the opposite side.

*Solve.* The ray has endpoint $B$ and direction through $C$, so it contains $B,C,D$ but not $A$.

*Look back.* $A$ lies on the ray opposite to $BC$, so it cannot be on the journey ray.

Therefore, $B,C,D$.
:::

## Common mistakes and connections

:::warning What can go wrong?
- **Reversing a ray name.** Assuming ray $AB$ always equals ray $BA$. The rays have different endpoints and usually opposite directions. Read the first letter as the endpoint before comparing.
- **Using “between” for non-collinear points.** $B$ looks visually near the middle of $A,C$, so it is called between. Betweenness requires all three points to be collinear. Check collinearity first, then order.
:::

**Connections.** Points and lines form the basic language of geometry. Betweenness adds order, while a ray adds an endpoint and direction; these ideas prepare for segments, angles and coordinate systems. When solving a problem, separate three layers: the data, the mathematical relationship and the conclusion. Before substituting values, state why the chosen rule applies; after each step, check signs, units and conditions. A diagram, small table or simple case often reveals a missing assumption. Estimate before calculating so that you know whether the answer should be large or small, positive or negative. After obtaining a result, use an independent check: substitute it back into the data, measure it on the diagram, or test a boundary case. If one item of data changes, predict which part of the method changes and which part remains valid. This approach turns a formula into a connected system of reasons and makes the idea usable in an unfamiliar situation instead of only in a memorised exercise. It also produces written work that another student can follow, question and verify line by line.

## Summary

- Exactly one line passes through two distinct points.
- Betweenness applies only to three collinear points.
- Ray $AB$ has endpoint $A$ and passes through $B$.
- Ray names are ordered; ray $AB$ usually differs from ray $BA$.
- Opposite rays share an endpoint and form a line.
