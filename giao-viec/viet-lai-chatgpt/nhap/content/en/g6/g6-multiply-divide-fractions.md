A hypothetical garden uses $\dfrac35$ of its area for vegetables; $\dfrac23$ of that part grows leafy vegetables. The fraction of the whole garden is “$\dfrac23$ of $\dfrac35$”, leading to fraction multiplication. If $\dfrac34$ of a route is $18$ km, which inverse operation finds the whole route?

## multiplying and dividing fractions

**A short exploration.**
In a unit rectangle, take $\dfrac35$ horizontally and then $\dfrac23$ of that strip vertically. The overlap is $\dfrac35\cdot\dfrac23=\dfrac6{15}=\dfrac25$ of the original rectangle. The picture shows that “of” corresponds to multiplication.

:::definition Multiplying fractions
For $b,d\ne0$, $\dfrac ab\cdot\dfrac cd=\dfrac{ac}{bd}$. Common factors may be cancelled across numerator and denominator before multiplication.
:::

:::definition Dividing fractions
To divide by a fraction other than $0$, multiply by its reciprocal: $\dfrac ab\div\dfrac cd=\dfrac ab\cdot\dfrac dc$, where $c,d\ne0$.
:::

**Why does this work?**
Multiplication finds a fraction of a fraction, so the counts in the numerators multiply and the subdivisions in the denominators multiply. Division asks how many copies of the divisor fit in the dividend. Multiplying by the reciprocal reverses multiplication because $\dfrac cd\cdot\dfrac dc=1$, cancelling the factor $\dfrac cd$ while preserving the relation.

**How to check your own work.** For a question on this topic, first state in words what is given and what must be found. Next write the rule you will use, substitute values or reason one step at a time, and then check the result in a different way: put it back into the data, estimate its size, or compare it with a diagram or number line. If two methods disagree, locate the first step where they differ instead of changing only the final answer. This habit catches sign errors, missing units and overlooked cases.

**Systems view.** multiplying and dividing fractions is not an isolated trick. Follow the chain: the data determine a model, the model determines the calculation, and the result must return to the original situation. When one piece of data changes, predict whether the result should increase, decrease or stay unchanged before calculating again.

## Worked examples and applications

:::example Multiplying with cancellation
Calculate $\dfrac{-7}{12}\cdot\dfrac{18}{35}$.

**Solution.**
Cancel $7$ with $35$ and $18$ with $12$: $\dfrac{-7}{12}\cdot\dfrac{18}{35}=-\dfrac{1\cdot3}{2\cdot5}=-\dfrac3{10}$.

Therefore, $-\dfrac3{10}$.
:::

:::example Dividing two fractions
Calculate $\dfrac56\div\dfrac{-10}{9}$.

**Solution.**
$\dfrac56\div\dfrac{-10}{9}=\dfrac56\cdot\dfrac9{-10}=-\dfrac{45}{60}=-\dfrac34$.

Therefore, $-\dfrac34$.
:::

:::example Order of operations
Calculate $\dfrac23-\dfrac34\cdot\dfrac89$.

**Solution.**
Multiply first: $\dfrac34\cdot\dfrac89=\dfrac23$. Hence $\dfrac23-\dfrac23=0$.

Therefore, $0$.
:::

:::example Vegetable area
$\dfrac35$ of a garden grows vegetables; $\dfrac23$ of that part grows leafy vegetables. What fraction of the whole garden grows leafy vegetables?

**Solution.**

*Understand.* Find a fraction of a fraction rather than adding the fractions.

*Plan.* Use the product $\dfrac23\cdot\dfrac35$.

*Solve.* The required fraction is $\dfrac23\cdot\dfrac35=\dfrac25$.

*Look back.* $\dfrac25<\dfrac35$, as leafy vegetables use only part of the vegetable area. If all vegetables were leafy, the result would be $\dfrac35$.

Therefore, leafy vegetables use $\dfrac25$ of the garden.
:::

## Common mistakes and connections

:::warning What can go wrong?
- **Cross-multiplying in a product.** Wrong: $\dfrac23\cdot\dfrac45=\dfrac{2\cdot5}{3\cdot4}$. Multiplication uses numerator times numerator and denominator times denominator. $\dfrac23\cdot\dfrac45=\dfrac8{15}$.
- **Inverting the dividend.** Wrong: $\dfrac23\div\dfrac45=\dfrac32\cdot\dfrac45$. Only the divisor is inverted. $\dfrac23\div\dfrac45=\dfrac23\cdot\dfrac54=\dfrac56$.
:::

**Connections.** Fraction multiplication describes “a fraction of a fraction”, while division reverses multiplication. Before calculating, predict the size of the result. Multiplying a positive number by a positive fraction below $1$ must make it smaller; dividing by a positive fraction below $1$ must make it larger. This prediction quickly exposes an incorrectly inverted fraction. During cancellation, divide one factor in a numerator and one factor in a denominator by the same number other than $0$; never cancel across addition or subtraction. First write the step that changes division into multiplication by the reciprocal, and only then cancel common factors. An area model gives an independent check for multiplication, while multiplying the quotient by the divisor checks a division. These habits are useful later in proportions, speed, rates of work, area and equations. They also make every transformation visible, so a sign error or an illegal cancellation can be located at the line where it first appears. A final check should include the sign, the expected size and a reverse calculation. For division, multiply the proposed quotient by the original divisor; for multiplication, estimate with nearby whole numbers. Writing units beside real quantities also prevents a bare fraction from being mistaken for a length, time or area. These checks turn a correct rule into a reliable method.

## Summary

- Multiply numerators and multiply denominators.
- Divide by multiplying by the reciprocal of the divisor.
- Division by $0$ is not allowed.
- Cancelling before multiplication keeps calculations smaller.
- Without brackets, multiply or divide before adding or subtracting.
