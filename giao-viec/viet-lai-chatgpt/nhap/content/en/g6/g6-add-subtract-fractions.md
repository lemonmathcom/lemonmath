A hypothetical tank is $\dfrac38$ full and then receives another $\dfrac14$ of its capacity. To find the new amount, we cannot add $3+1$ and $8+4$ because eighths and quarters have different sizes. What must we do first?

## adding and subtracting fractions

**A short exploration.**
Write $\dfrac14=\dfrac28$, so $\dfrac38+\dfrac14=\dfrac38+\dfrac28=\dfrac58$. A common denominator only renames the parts; it does not change the amount. For subtraction, use a common denominator and then subtract numerators.

:::definition Adding fractions
For equal denominators, $\dfrac ab+\dfrac cb=\dfrac{a+c}{b}$ ($b\ne0$). For different denominators, write the fractions over one positive common denominator and add the numerators.
:::

:::definition Subtracting fractions
Use $\dfrac ab-\dfrac cd=\dfrac ab+\left(-\dfrac cd\right)$. After finding a common denominator, subtract the numerators, keep the denominator and simplify.
:::

**Why does this work?**
Only equal-sized parts can be added or subtracted directly. A common denominator creates one unit part; the number of parts is combined in the numerator while the part size in the denominator remains fixed. This also explains why adding denominators is wrong.

**How to check your own work.** For a question on this topic, first state in words what is given and what must be found. Next write the rule you will use, substitute values or reason one step at a time, and then check the result in a different way: put it back into the data, estimate its size, or compare it with a diagram or number line. If two methods disagree, locate the first step where they differ instead of changing only the final answer. This habit catches sign errors, missing units and overlooked cases.

**Systems view.** adding and subtracting fractions is not an isolated trick. Follow the chain: the data determine a model, the model determines the calculation, and the result must return to the original situation. When one piece of data changes, predict whether the result should increase, decrease or stay unchanged before calculating again.

## Worked examples and applications

:::example Fractions with the same denominator
Calculate $\dfrac7{15}+\dfrac{-11}{15}$.

**Solution.**
$\dfrac7{15}+\dfrac{-11}{15}=\dfrac{7-11}{15}=-\dfrac4{15}$.

Therefore, $-\dfrac4{15}$.
:::

:::example Fractions with different denominators
Calculate $\dfrac56-\dfrac7{10}$.

**Solution.**
The common denominator is $30$: $\dfrac56-\dfrac7{10}=\dfrac{25}{30}-\dfrac{21}{30}=\dfrac4{30}=\dfrac2{15}$.

Therefore, $\dfrac2{15}$.
:::

:::example Adding mixed numbers
Calculate $2\dfrac13+1\dfrac56$.

**Solution.**
$2\dfrac13=\dfrac73$ and $1\dfrac56=\dfrac{11}{6}$. Hence the sum is $\dfrac{14}{6}+\dfrac{11}{6}=\dfrac{25}{6}=4\dfrac16$.

Therefore, $4\dfrac16$.
:::

:::example Water in a tank
A tank is $\dfrac38$ full and receives another $\dfrac14$ of its capacity. What fraction is now filled?

**Solution.**

*Understand.* Both amounts are fractions of the whole tank capacity.

*Plan.* Use denominator $8$ and then add numerators.

*Solve.* $\dfrac14=\dfrac28$, so $\dfrac38+\dfrac28=\dfrac58$.

*Look back.* $\dfrac58<1$, so the tank is not full, and the increase is exactly $\dfrac28$. Another $\dfrac38$ would fill the tank.

Therefore, the tank is $\dfrac58$ full.
:::

## Common mistakes and connections

:::warning What can go wrong?
- **Adding both denominators.** Wrong: $\dfrac12+\dfrac13=\dfrac25$. Halves and thirds do not have the same size. Correct: $\dfrac12+\dfrac13=\dfrac36+\dfrac26=\dfrac56$.
- **Losing a sign when subtracting a negative.** Wrong: $\dfrac25-\left(-\dfrac15\right)=\dfrac15$. Subtracting a negative means adding its opposite. $\dfrac25-\left(-\dfrac15\right)=\dfrac35$.
:::

**Connections.** Fraction addition and subtraction extend integer addition: first use the same unit. Common denominators also support comparing ratios, working with time and length, and solving problems with several successive parts. A useful mental model is to imagine every fraction as a collection of equal pieces on one unit strip. Before combining the pieces, rename them so that every piece has the same size. This picture explains the calculation instead of turning it into a rule to memorise. Estimation gives another strong check. The sum of two positive fractions must be greater than either addend, while a positive difference, when the first fraction is larger, must lie between $0$ and that first fraction. If an answer violates these bounds, inspect the common denominator and the signs before doing more arithmetic. For a calculation with several terms, write the chosen common denominator on one line, rewrite every fraction on the next line, and combine numerators only after checking that no term was omitted. Simplify at the end unless early cancellation is completely transparent. A number-line sketch or a unit rectangle can provide an independent visual check. These habits matter beyond this lesson: the same control of units appears in time calculations, measurements, rates and later algebra. Clear written steps also make it possible to locate the first incorrect transformation rather than changing a final answer without understanding the cause. It also makes peer review easier because each choice can be discussed and checked independently.

## Summary

- Same denominator: add or subtract numerators and keep the denominator.
- Different denominators: use a common denominator first.
- Subtracting a fraction means adding its opposite.
- It is usually easier to convert mixed numbers to fractions first.
- Simplify the result and check its sign.
