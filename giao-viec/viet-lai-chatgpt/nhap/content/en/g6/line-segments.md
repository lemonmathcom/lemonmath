A straight path joining markers $A,B$ is $12$ m long. Unlike a line extending indefinitely or a ray with one endpoint, this part has two fixed endpoints. How do we describe this finite part and compare its length?

## line segments and their lengths

**A short exploration.**
Mark $A,B$ on a line and highlight the part from $A$ to $B$. This is segment $AB$; the notation $AB$ also denotes its length when the context is clear. If $M$ lies between $A,B$, measurement gives $AM+MB=AB$.

:::definition Line segment
Segment $AB$ consists of points $A,B$ and every point between them. Points $A,B$ are its endpoints; segments $AB$ and $BA$ are the same segment.
:::

:::definition Length and additivity
Length $AB$ is the non-negative number measuring the distance between $A,B$. If $M$ is between $A,B$, then $AM+MB=AB$. Conversely, for three collinear points, this equality confirms that $M$ is between $A,B$.
:::

**Why does this work?**
A segment is made from non-overlapping parts $AM$ and $MB$, so their lengths add to the whole. Length does not depend on the naming order: $AB=BA$, although rays $AB$ and $BA$ may differ.

If two methods disagree, locate the first step where they differ instead of changing only the final answer. This habit catches sign errors, missing units and overlooked cases.

## Worked examples and applications

:::example Finding a remaining part
$M$ lies between $A,B$, $AB=17$ cm and $AM=6$ cm. Find $MB$.

**Solution.**
$MB=AB-AM=17-6=11$ cm.

Therefore, $11$ cm.
:::

:::example Checking betweenness
Collinear points $P,Q,R$ satisfy $PQ=4$ cm, $QR=9$ cm and $PR=13$ cm. Which point is between the other two?

**Solution.**
Since $PQ+QR=4+9=13=PR$, point $Q$ lies between $P,R$.

Therefore, $Q$.
:::

:::example Comparing segments
Let $CD=7.5$ cm and $EF=75$ mm. Compare $CD,EF$.

**Solution.**
$75$ mm $=7.5$ cm, so $CD=EF$.

Therefore, $CD=EF$.
:::

:::example A three-part route
On a line, the points occur in order $A,M,N,B$. Given $AM=3$ km, $MN=5$ km and $NB=4$ km, find $AB$.

**Solution.**

*Understand.* Record the given data, the required result and any conditions.

*Plan.* Choose the relevant definition or rule, then work in a logical order.

*Solve.* The parts meet only at endpoints, so $AB=AM+MN+NB=3+5+4=12$ km.

*Look back.* Compare the result with the data and use an independent check.

Therefore, $12$ km.
:::

## Common mistakes and connections

:::warning What can go wrong?
- **Confusing a segment with a ray.** Assuming segment $AB$ continues indefinitely through $B$. A segment has two endpoints and is finite. Distinguish segment $AB$ from ray $AB$ by the number of endpoints.
- **Adding lengths with the wrong order.** Always writing $AC=AB+BC$ for any three points. The equality holds only when $B$ lies between $A,C$. Identify the point between the other two before adding.
:::

**Connections.** A segment turns infinite geometric lines into a measurable object. Additivity of length supports perimeter, midpoints, constructions and distance on a number line. When solving a problem, separate three layers: the data, the mathematical relationship and the conclusion. Before substituting values, state why the chosen rule applies; after each step, check signs, units and conditions. A diagram, small table or simple case often reveals a missing assumption. Estimate before calculating so that you know whether the answer should be large or small, positive or negative. After obtaining a result, use an independent check: substitute it back into the data, measure it on the diagram, or test a boundary case. If one item of data changes, predict which part of the method changes and which part remains valid. This approach turns a formula into a connected system of reasons and makes the idea usable in an unfamiliar situation instead of only in a memorised exercise. It also produces written work that another student can follow, question and verify line by line.

## Summary

- Segment $AB$ has endpoints $A,B$.
- $AB=BA$ when the notation denotes length.
- If $M$ is between $A,B$, then $AM+MB=AB$.
- Convert to the same unit before comparing or adding.
- Point order determines whether to add or subtract.
