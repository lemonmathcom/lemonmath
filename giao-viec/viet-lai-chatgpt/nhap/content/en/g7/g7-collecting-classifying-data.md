A school wants to estimate study time for all students. Asking only those in the library is convenient but may give values that are too high. How should a sound data plan define the population, sample and variable?

## planning collection, classification and quality checks for data

**A short exploration.**
Define the population as the whole school, sample across year groups and classes, and ask one consistent question about the same time period. Minutes are numerical data; they may then be grouped as $0$–under $30$, $30$–under $60$, and $60$ minutes or more.

:::definition Population, sample and variable
The population is the full group of interest; a sample is the observed subset. A variable is the feature recorded for each unit. Primary data are collected directly, while secondary data come from an existing source.
:::

:::definition Classification and frequency tables
Categorical data use named groups; numerical data may be discrete or continuous and can be grouped into intervals. Each value belongs to one class, classes cover the required range, and total frequency equals sample size.
:::

**Why does this work?**
A representative sample reflects important population groups rather than only a convenient group. A consistent measuring procedure reduces systematic error. Clear interval boundaries prevent double counting; grouping loses detail, so class width should suit the question.

**How to check your own work.** For a question on this topic, first state in words what is given and what must be found. Next write the rule you will use, substitute values or reason one step at a time, and then check the result in a different way: put it back into the data, estimate its size, or compare it with a diagram or number line. If two methods disagree, locate the first step where they differ instead of changing only the final answer. This habit catches sign errors, missing units and overlooked cases.

**Systems view.** planning collection, classification and quality checks for data is not an isolated trick. Follow the chain: the data determine a model, the model determines the calculation, and the result must return to the original situation. When one piece of data changes, predict whether the result should increase, decrease or stay unchanged before calculating again.

## Worked examples and applications

:::example Identifying the population
A survey of $120$ students is used to infer about all $900$ students in a school. What is the population?

**Solution.**
The population is all $900$ students; the $120$ students form the sample.

Therefore, $900$ students.
:::

:::example Classifying a variable
Is “time to run $100$ m in seconds” categorical, discrete or continuous?

**Solution.**
Time can take any value in an interval, so it is continuous numerical data.

Therefore, continuous.
:::

:::example Finding sample size
A frequency table has group frequencies $14,18,11,7$. Find the sample size.

**Solution.**
$14+18+11+7=50$.

Therefore, $50$.
:::

:::example A simple stratified sample
A school has $60\%$ lower-year and $40\%$ upper-year students. In a sample of $100$, how many from each group preserve the proportions?

**Solution.**

*Understand.* Record the given data, the required result and any conditions.

*Plan.* Choose the relevant definition or rule, then work in a logical order.

*Solve.* Select $60$ from lower years and $40$ from upper years.

*Look back.* Compare the result with the data and use an independent check.

Therefore, $60;40$.
:::

## Common mistakes and connections

:::warning What can go wrong?
- **Confusing sample and population.** Calling $50$ respondents the entire group of interest. A sample is only the observed part of the population. State sample size and population size separately.
- **Using classes that are too wide.** Putting all values into one or two broad classes. Important patterns and differences become hidden. Choose enough detail without making too many sparse classes.
:::

**Connections.** The quality of analysis depends on the quality of collection. Population, sample and variable prepare for statistical inference; frequency tables prepare for charts and summary measures. When solving a problem, separate three layers: the data, the mathematical relationship and the conclusion. Before substituting values, state why the chosen rule applies; after each step, check signs, units and conditions. A diagram, small table or simple case often reveals a missing assumption. Estimate before calculating so that you know whether the answer should be large or small, positive or negative. After obtaining a result, use an independent check: substitute it back into the data, measure it on the diagram, or test a boundary case. If one item of data changes, predict which part of the method changes and which part remains valid. This approach turns a formula into a connected system of reasons and makes the idea usable in an unfamiliar situation instead of only in a memorised exercise. It also produces written work that another student can follow, question and verify line by line.

## Summary

- The population is the group of interest; a sample is observed.
- Define the variable and measurement method before collecting.
- A representative sample reduces selection bias.
- Classes must be clear, non-overlapping and cover the data.
- Total frequency equals sample size.
