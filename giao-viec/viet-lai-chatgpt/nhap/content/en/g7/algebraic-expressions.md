A hypothetical journey has a $25$-thousand-dong starting fee and costs $8$ thousand per kilometre. For distance $x$ km, cost is $25+8x$. This expression stores the rule for every journey rather than one calculation.

## variables, terms, coefficients and values of algebraic expressions

**A short exploration.**
Substituting $x=6$ into $25+8x$ gives $25+8\cdot6=73$. Symbol $x$ is a variable; $25$ and $8x$ are terms; $8$ is the coefficient of $x$. The expression structure remains when another value is substituted.

:::definition Expressions and terms
An algebraic expression contains numbers, variables and operations. Parts separated by top-level addition or subtraction are terms. In term $-5x^2$, coefficient is $-5$ and variable part is $x^2$.
:::

:::definition Value and like terms
An expression value is found by substituting variables and using the order of operations. Like terms have the same variable part with the same exponents; they combine by adding coefficients.
:::

**Why does this work?**
A variable allows one symbol to represent many values. Like terms measure the same algebraic “unit”, as $3$ metres plus $5$ metres gives $8$ metres; $3x$ and $5x^2$ have different variable parts and cannot combine.

**How to check your own work.** For a question on this topic, first state in words what is given and what must be found. Next write the rule you will use, substitute values or reason one step at a time, and then check the result in a different way: put it back into the data, estimate its size, or compare it with a diagram or number line. If two methods disagree, locate the first step where they differ instead of changing only the final answer. This habit catches sign errors, missing units and overlooked cases.

**Systems view.** variables, terms, coefficients and values of algebraic expressions is not an isolated trick. Follow the chain: the data determine a model, the model determines the calculation, and the result must return to the original situation. When one piece of data changes, predict whether the result should increase, decrease or stay unchanged before calculating again.

## Worked examples and applications

:::example Identifying terms
State the terms of $4x^2-3x+7$.

**Solution.**
Keep each attached sign: the terms are $4x^2,-3x,7$.

Therefore, $4x^2,-3x,7$.
:::

:::example Finding a coefficient
What is the coefficient of $x^3$ in $-9x^3+2x$?

**Solution.**
The term containing $x^3$ is $-9x^3$, so its coefficient is $-9$.

Therefore, $-9$.
:::

:::example Evaluating an expression
Evaluate $2x^2-3x+1$ at $x=-2$.

**Solution.**
$2(-2)^2-3(-2)+1=8+6+1=15$.

Therefore, $15$.
:::

:::example Writing a cost expression
A hypothetical service charges $40$ thousand dong plus $6$ thousand per hour $h$. Write the cost and find it for $5$ hours.

**Solution.**

*Understand.* Record the given data, the required result and any conditions.

*Plan.* Choose the relevant definition or rule, then work in a logical order.

*Solve.* $C=40+6h$; when $h=5$, $C=40+30=70$ thousand dong.

*Look back.* Compare the result with the data and use an independent check.

Therefore, $70$ thousand dong.
:::

## Common mistakes and connections

:::warning What can go wrong?
- **Dropping brackets around a negative substitution.** Substituting $x=-2$ into $x^2$ as $-2^2=-4$. The variable value is the entire negative number and needs brackets. Write $(-2)^2=4$.
- **Combining unlike terms.** Writing $3x+2x^2=5x^3$. The variable parts and exponents differ. Keep $2x^2+3x$ or combine only within like groups.
:::

**Connections.** Algebraic expressions turn verbal rules into models that can be evaluated, compared and transformed. They form the language of equations, functions, geometric formulas and polynomials. When solving a problem, separate three layers: the data, the mathematical relationship and the conclusion. Before substituting values, state why the chosen rule applies; after each step, check signs, units and conditions. A diagram, small table or simple case often reveals a missing assumption. Estimate before calculating so that you know whether the answer should be large or small, positive or negative. After obtaining a result, use an independent check: substitute it back into the data, measure it on the diagram, or test a boundary case. If one item of data changes, predict which part of the method changes and which part remains valid. This approach turns a formula into a connected system of reasons and makes the idea usable in an unfamiliar situation instead of only in a memorised exercise. It also produces written work that another student can follow, question and verify line by line.

## Summary

- Terms are separated by top-level addition or subtraction.
- A coefficient includes the sign of its term.
- Use brackets when substituting a negative number.
- Only like terms can be combined.
- Distribution multiplies a factor by every term in brackets.
