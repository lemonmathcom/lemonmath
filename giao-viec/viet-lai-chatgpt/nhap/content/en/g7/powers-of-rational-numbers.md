A square has side $\dfrac32$ m, so its area is $\left(\dfrac32\right)^2$ m². Power notation shortens repeated multiplication, but how can a negative sign and brackets change the result?

## powers of rational numbers and exponent laws

**A short exploration.**
 $\left(-\dfrac23\right)^3=\left(-\dfrac23\right)\left(-\dfrac23\right)\left(-\dfrac23\right)=-\dfrac8{27}$. Three negative factors give a negative result; with an even exponent, negative signs pair and the result is positive.

:::definition Power
For a positive natural number $n$, $a^n$ is the product of $n$ factors equal to $a$. The number $a$ is the base and $n$ the exponent. For $a\ne0$, define $a^0=1$.
:::

:::definition Exponent laws
For a common base: $a^m\cdot a^n=a^{m+n}$ and $a^m\div a^n=a^{m-n}$ when $a\ne0,m\ge n$. Also, $(a^m)^n=a^{mn}$ and $(ab)^n=a^nb^n$.
:::

**Why does this work?**
Exponent laws come from counting factors. Multiplying two products with the same base gives $m+n$ factors; division cancels common factors. Brackets determine whether a negative sign belongs to the base.

**How to check your own work.** For a question on this topic, first state in words what is given and what must be found. Next write the rule you will use, substitute values or reason one step at a time, and then check the result in a different way: put it back into the data, estimate its size, or compare it with a diagram or number line. If two methods disagree, locate the first step where they differ instead of changing only the final answer. This habit catches sign errors, missing units and overlooked cases.

**Systems view.** powers of rational numbers and exponent laws is not an isolated trick. Follow the chain: the data determine a model, the model determines the calculation, and the result must return to the original situation. When one piece of data changes, predict whether the result should increase, decrease or stay unchanged before calculating again.

## Worked examples and applications

:::example Evaluating a fractional power
Evaluate $\left(\dfrac34\right)^3$.

**Solution.**
$\left(\dfrac34\right)^3=\dfrac{3^3}{4^3}=\dfrac{27}{64}$.

Therefore, $\dfrac{27}{64}$.
:::

:::example A negative base with even exponent
Evaluate $\left(-\dfrac25\right)^4$.

**Solution.**
The even exponent gives a positive result: $\left(-\dfrac25\right)^4=\dfrac{16}{625}$.

Therefore, $\dfrac{16}{625}$.
:::

:::example Multiplying a common base
Simplify $\left(\dfrac57\right)^3\cdot\left(\dfrac57\right)^4$.

**Solution.**
Add exponents: $\left(\dfrac57\right)^{3+4}=\left(\dfrac57\right)^7$.

Therefore, $\left(\dfrac57\right)^7$.
:::

:::example Area after scaling
A square of side $6$ m is scaled by $\dfrac12$. What fraction of the old area is the new area?

**Solution.**

*Understand.* Record the given data, the required result and any conditions.

*Plan.* Choose the relevant definition or rule, then work in a logical order.

*Solve.* Area changes by the square of the scale factor: $\left(\dfrac12\right)^2=\dfrac14$.

*Look back.* Compare the result with the data and use an independent check.

Therefore, $\dfrac14$.
:::

## Common mistakes and connections

:::warning What can go wrong?
- **Multiplying exponents when multiplying powers.** Writing $a^2\cdot a^3=a^6$. The combined product has $2+3$ factors, not $2\cdot3$. The correct result is $a^2\cdot a^3=a^5$.
- **Dropping brackets around a negative base.** Treating $(-3)^2$ as equal to $-3^2$. In the second expression, the exponent applies only to $3$. $(-3)^2=9$ but $-3^2=-9$.
:::

**Connections.** Powers describe repeated growth and area or volume scaling while shortening long products. Exponent laws later support scientific notation, polynomials and exponential functions. They also make patterns across many repeated calculations much easier to compare, communicate and verify. When solving a problem, separate three layers: the data, the mathematical relationship and the conclusion. Before substituting values, state why the chosen rule applies; after each step, check signs, units and conditions. A diagram, small table or simple case often reveals a missing assumption. Estimate before calculating so that you know whether the answer should be large or small, positive or negative. After obtaining a result, use an independent check: substitute it back into the data, measure it on the diagram, or test a boundary case. If one item of data changes, predict which part of the method changes and which part remains valid. This approach turns a formula into a connected system of reasons and makes the idea usable in an unfamiliar situation instead of only in a memorised exercise. It also produces written work that another student can follow, question and verify line by line.

## Summary

- $a^n$ is a product of $n$ factors equal to $a$.
- A negative base gives a positive result for an even exponent.
- $a^m\cdot a^n=a^{m+n}$.
- $(a^m)^n=a^{mn}$.
- Brackets determine whether a negative sign belongs to the base.
