A hypothetical account changes by $-\dfrac34$ million dong and then by $\dfrac56$ million dong. To find the net change, we add rational numbers with unlike signs; which rules keep the sign and magnitude correct?

## addition, subtraction, multiplication and division of rational numbers

**A short exploration.**
Use denominator $12$: $-\dfrac34=-\dfrac9{12}$ and $\dfrac56=\dfrac{10}{12}$. Their sum is $\dfrac1{12}$. On a number line, moving left $\dfrac34$ then right $\dfrac56$ also ends $\dfrac1{12}$ to the right of $0$.

:::definition Addition and subtraction
To add fractions, use a common denominator and add numerators. Subtraction becomes addition of the opposite: $a-b=a+(-b)$. For unlike signs, the sign follows the number with greater absolute value.
:::

:::definition Multiplication and division
$\dfrac ab\cdot\dfrac cd=\dfrac{ac}{bd}$. To divide by a rational different from $0$, multiply by its reciprocal: $x\div y=x\cdot\dfrac1y$. Sign rules are the same as for integers.
:::

**Why does this work?**
A common denominator divides the unit into equal parts, making numerators addable or subtractable. The reciprocal works in division because $y\cdot\dfrac1y=1$ when $y\ne0$. Cancelling before multiplication keeps numbers small without changing value.

**How to check your own work.** For a question on this topic, first state in words what is given and what must be found. Next write the rule you will use, substitute values or reason one step at a time, and then check the result in a different way: put it back into the data, estimate its size, or compare it with a diagram or number line. If two methods disagree, locate the first step where they differ instead of changing only the final answer. This habit catches sign errors, missing units and overlooked cases.

**Systems view.** addition, subtraction, multiplication and division of rational numbers is not an isolated trick. Follow the chain: the data determine a model, the model determines the calculation, and the result must return to the original situation. When one piece of data changes, predict whether the result should increase, decrease or stay unchanged before calculating again.

## Worked examples and applications

:::example Adding unlike denominators
Calculate $-\dfrac23+\dfrac56$.

**Solution.**
$-\dfrac23+\dfrac56=-\dfrac46+\dfrac56=\dfrac16$.

Therefore, $\dfrac16$.
:::

:::example Subtracting a negative
Calculate $\dfrac45-\left(-\dfrac3{10}\right)$.

**Solution.**
$\dfrac45+\dfrac3{10}=\dfrac8{10}+\dfrac3{10}=\dfrac{11}{10}$.

Therefore, $\dfrac{11}{10}$.
:::

:::example Multiplying with cancellation
Calculate $-\dfrac{14}{15}\cdot\dfrac{25}{21}$.

**Solution.**
Cancel $14$ with $21$ and $25$ with $15$: the result is $-\dfrac{2\cdot5}{3\cdot3}=-\dfrac{10}{9}$.

Therefore, $-\dfrac{10}{9}$.
:::

:::example Changing water level
A water level falls $1.4$ m, rises $\dfrac35$ m and falls $0.2$ m. Find the net change.

**Solution.**

*Understand.* Record the given data, the required result and any conditions.

*Plan.* Choose the relevant definition or rule, then work in a logical order.

*Solve.* $-1.4+0.6-0.2=-1.0$ m, so the net change is a fall of $1$ m.

*Look back.* Compare the result with the data and use an independent check.

Therefore, $-1$ m.
:::

## Common mistakes and connections

:::warning What can go wrong?
- **Adding denominators.** Writing $\dfrac12+\dfrac13=\dfrac25$. The parts have different sizes, so numerators cannot yet be added. Use denominator $6$: $\dfrac36+\dfrac26=\dfrac56$.
- **Forgetting to invert the divisor.** Multiplying directly when calculating $\dfrac23\div\dfrac45$. Division by a number is multiplication by its reciprocal. Write $\dfrac23\cdot\dfrac54=\dfrac56$.
:::

**Connections.** The four rational operations model changes, parts of quantities, rates and ratios. Commutative, associative and distributive properties allow efficient transformations while preserving value. When solving a problem, separate three layers: the data, the mathematical relationship and the conclusion. Before substituting values, state why the chosen rule applies; after each step, check signs, units and conditions. A diagram, small table or simple case often reveals a missing assumption. Estimate before calculating so that you know whether the answer should be large or small, positive or negative. After obtaining a result, use an independent check: substitute it back into the data, measure it on the diagram, or test a boundary case. If one item of data changes, predict which part of the method changes and which part remains valid. This approach turns a formula into a connected system of reasons and makes the idea usable in an unfamiliar situation instead of only in a memorised exercise. It also produces written work that another student can follow, question and verify line by line.

## Summary

- Adding and subtracting fractions requires a common denominator.
- $a-b=a+(-b)$.
- Multiply numerators and denominators, then simplify.
- Dividing by a number different from $0$ means multiplying by its reciprocal.
- Estimate sign and magnitude before calculating.
