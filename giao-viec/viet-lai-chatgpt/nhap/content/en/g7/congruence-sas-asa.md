## SAS and ASA triangle congruence

**A short exploration.**
Fix side $AB$, construct at $A$ a ray making the given angle with $AB$, then mark the given length $AC$ on the ray. Point $C$ is unique. Similarly, one side and the angles at its endpoints define two rays meeting at the third vertex.

:::definition Side–angle–side
If two sides and the included angle of one triangle equal the corresponding parts of another, the triangles are congruent by SAS. The angle must lie between the stated sides.
:::

:::definition Angle–side–angle
If one side and the two angles adjacent to it in one triangle equal the corresponding side and angles of another, the triangles are congruent by ASA.
:::

**Why does this work?**
In SAS, two lengths locate endpoints on two rays with a fixed angle, so the third side cannot vary. In ASA, the known side is a base and its endpoint angles determine the directions of the remaining sides; the rays have a unique intersection.

**How to check your own work.** For a question on this topic, first state in words what is given and what must be found. Next write the rule you will use, substitute values or reason one step at a time, and then check the result in a different way: put it back into the data, estimate its size, or compare it with a diagram or number line. If two methods disagree, locate the first step where they differ instead of changing only the final answer. This habit catches sign errors, missing units and overlooked cases.

**Systems view.** SAS and ASA triangle congruence is not an isolated trick. Follow the chain: the data determine a model, the model determines the calculation, and the result must return to the original situation. When one piece of data changes, predict whether the result should increase, decrease or stay unchanged before calculating again.

## Worked examples and applications

:::example Recognising SAS
Given $AB=DE$, $AC=DF$ and $\angle A=\angle D$, which congruence case applies to $ABC,DEF$?

**Solution.**
$\angle A$ is included by $AB,AC$ and $\angle D$ by $DE,DF$, so SAS applies.

Therefore, SAS.
:::

:::example Recognising ASA
Given $\angle B=\angle E$, $BC=EF$, $\angle C=\angle F$, what follows?

**Solution.**
$BC,EF$ lie between the corresponding angles, so $\triangle BCA\cong\triangle EFD$ by ASA.

Therefore, congruent by ASA.
:::

:::example Finding a corresponding part
$\triangle ABC\cong\triangle PQR$ by SAS and $BC=12$ cm. Find $QR$.

**Solution.**
$BC$ corresponds to $QR$, so $QR=12$ cm.

Therefore, $12$ cm.
:::

:::example Indirect measurement
From point $O$, stakes $A,B$ are at distances $20$ m and $15$ m from $O$ and subtend $60^\circ$. A scale model uses corresponding sides and the same included angle. Why is its shape fixed?

**Solution.**

*Understand.* Record the given data, the required result and any conditions.

*Plan.* Choose the relevant definition or rule, then work in a logical order.

*Solve.* Two fixed side lengths at scale and their included angle determine the triangle by SAS; the model therefore has one corresponding shape.

*Look back.* Compare the result with the data and use an independent check.

Therefore, SAS.
:::

## Common mistakes and connections

:::warning What can go wrong?
- **Using a non-included angle for SAS.** Seeing two sides and any angle and declaring SAS. The angle must have the common endpoint of the two known sides. Circle the two sides and check that the angle lies between them.
- **Using the wrong side for ASA.** Pairing a side that is not between the stated angles. ASA here requires the side adjacent to both angles. Read angle–side–angle in order around the triangle.
:::

**Connections.** SAS and ASA show how minimal data can determine an entire triangle. They support indirect measurement, symmetry proofs and deductions of unmeasured sides or angles. When solving a problem, separate three layers: the data, the mathematical relationship and the conclusion. Before substituting values, state why the chosen rule applies; after each step, check signs, units and conditions. A diagram, small table or simple case often reveals a missing assumption. Estimate before calculating so that you know whether the answer should be large or small, positive or negative. After obtaining a result, use an independent check: substitute it back into the data, measure it on the diagram, or test a boundary case. If one item of data changes, predict which part of the method changes and which part remains valid. This approach turns a formula into a connected system of reasons and makes the idea usable in an unfamiliar situation instead of only in a memorised exercise. It also produces written work that another student can follow, question and verify line by line.

## Summary

- SAS uses two corresponding sides and the included angle.
- ASA uses two angles and the side between them.
- Data must be paired in the correct corresponding order.
- A common side may be used as an equal side pair.
- Deduce corresponding parts after congruence is proved.
