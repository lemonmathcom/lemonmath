Hourly temperatures are $24,26,29,28,25^\circ$C. Joining points in time order reveals increases, decreases and rates of change. When is joining points meaningful, and what does a steep segment show?

## reading, drawing and analysing line graphs

**A short exploration.**
Put time on the horizontal axis and temperature on the vertical axis, choose uniform scales and plot five points. The segment from $26$ to $29$ rises $3^\circ$C; the next falls $1^\circ$C. Joining shows the trend between measurements when the quantity can vary continuously.

:::definition Line graph
A line graph displays value pairs as points joined in order, often chronological. Each axis needs a name, unit and scale; each point has coordinates corresponding to an observation.
:::

:::definition Trend and change
A rising segment shows increase, a falling segment decrease and a horizontal segment no change. Change equals final minus initial value; average rate of change equals change divided by elapsed time.
:::

**Why does this work?**
Time has a natural order, so joining points represents transition. Visual steepness is comparable only with fixed axis scales; changing intervals or truncating an axis can make a line look steeper without changing data. Separate categories should not be joined when intermediate values have no meaning.

**How to check your own work.** For a question on this topic, first state in words what is given and what must be found. Next write the rule you will use, substitute values or reason one step at a time, and then check the result in a different way: put it back into the data, estimate its size, or compare it with a diagram or number line. If two methods disagree, locate the first step where they differ instead of changing only the final answer. This habit catches sign errors, missing units and overlooked cases.

**Systems view.** reading, drawing and analysing line graphs is not an isolated trick. Follow the chain: the data determine a model, the model determines the calculation, and the result must return to the original situation. When one piece of data changes, predict whether the result should increase, decrease or stay unchanged before calculating again.

## Worked examples and applications

:::example Reading a point
What does point $(3,18)$ mean on a daily rainfall graph?

**Solution.**
If the horizontal axis is day and the vertical axis millimetres, rainfall on day $3$ is $18$ mm.

Therefore, day $3$: $18$ mm.
:::

:::example Finding an increase
A value rises from $42$ to $57$. What is the change?

**Solution.**
$57-42=15$, so the increase is $15$ units.

Therefore, $15$.
:::

:::example Rate of change
A water level rises from $1.2$ m to $2.1$ m in $3$ hours. Find the average rate of increase.

**Solution.**
$\dfrac{2.1-1.2}{3}=0.3$ m per hour.

Therefore, $0.3$ m per hour.
:::

:::example Tracking distance
A walker has cumulative distances $0,2,5,5,8$ km at hours $0,1,2,3,4$. When did the walker stop?

**Solution.**

*Understand.* Record the given data, the required result and any conditions.

*Plan.* Choose the relevant definition or rule, then work in a logical order.

*Solve.* From hour $2$ to hour $3$, distance stays at $5$ km, giving a horizontal segment; the walker stopped then.

*Look back.* Compare the result with the data and use an independent check.

Therefore, hours $2$–$3$.
:::

## Common mistakes and connections

:::warning What can go wrong?
- **Ignoring units and scale.** Reading grid spaces as actual values. Each interval may represent a value other than $1$. Read two consecutive tick labels and the unit first.
- **Joining unrelated categories.** Joining product categories as if intermediate values existed. A joining line implies order and continuous transition. Use a bar chart for separate categories.
:::

**Connections.** Line graphs highlight change over time and prepare for rate, slope and function concepts. Careful axis reading separates real trends from impressions created by design. When solving a problem, separate three layers: the data, the mathematical relationship and the conclusion. Before substituting values, state why the chosen rule applies; after each step, check signs, units and conditions. A diagram, small table or simple case often reveals a missing assumption. Estimate before calculating so that you know whether the answer should be large or small, positive or negative. After obtaining a result, use an independent check: substitute it back into the data, measure it on the diagram, or test a boundary case. If one item of data changes, predict which part of the method changes and which part remains valid. This approach turns a formula into a connected system of reasons and makes the idea usable in an unfamiliar situation instead of only in a memorised exercise. It also produces written work that another student can follow, question and verify line by line.

## Summary

- A point represents a pair of values on two axes.
- Join points when order and intermediate values are meaningful.
- A rising segment shows increase; a falling one shows decrease.
- Average rate equals change divided by time.
- Always check scale, units and axis starting point.
