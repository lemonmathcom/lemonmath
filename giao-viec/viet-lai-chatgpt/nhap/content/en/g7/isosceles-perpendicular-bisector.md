A point $P$ is equidistant from markers $A,B$. Joining $PA,PB$ forms an isosceles triangle; at the same time, $P$ lies on the perpendicular line through the midpoint of $AB$. How are these descriptions connected?

## properties of isosceles triangles and perpendicular bisectors

**A short exploration.**
Fold paper so that $A$ coincides with $B$. The crease is perpendicular to $AB$ at its midpoint, and every point on it is equidistant from $A,B$. Choosing $P$ on the crease gives $PA=PB$, so $\triangle PAB$ is isosceles with equal base angles.

:::definition Isosceles triangle
An isosceles triangle has two equal sides, called legs; the remaining side is the base and the common endpoint of the legs is the vertex. Its base angles are equal; conversely, equal angles have equal opposite sides.
:::

:::definition Perpendicular bisector
The perpendicular bisector of segment $AB$ is perpendicular to $AB$ at its midpoint. A point lies on it if and only if it is equidistant from $A,B$.
:::

**Why does this work?**
If $PA=PB$ and $M$ is the midpoint of $AB$, triangles $PMA,PMB$ have three equal side pairs and are congruent by SSS. Their equal angles at $M$ form a linear pair, so each is $90^\circ$; hence $PM$ is the perpendicular bisector. The converse follows through the same right triangles.

## Worked examples and applications

:::example Base angles
Triangle $ABC$ is isosceles at $A$ and $\angle A=44^\circ$. Find $\angle B,\angle C$.

**Solution.**
The base angles are equal; each is $(180^\circ-44^\circ)\div2=68^\circ$.

Therefore, $68^\circ,68^\circ$.
:::

:::example Recognising isosceles
In a triangle, $\angle B=\angle C$. What follows about the sides?

**Solution.**
Sides opposite equal angles are equal: $AC=AB$, so the triangle is isosceles at $A$.

Therefore, $AB=AC$.
:::

:::example A point on a perpendicular bisector
$P$ lies on the perpendicular bisector of $AB$ and $PA=7.2$ cm. Find $PB$.

**Solution.**
A point on a perpendicular bisector is equidistant from the endpoints, so $PB=PA=7.2$ cm.

Therefore, $7.2$ cm.
:::

:::example Placing an equidistant station
Hypothetical villages are at $A,B$. Where may a station be placed to be equidistant from them?

**Solution.**

*Understand.* Record the given data, the required result and any conditions.

*Plan.* Choose the relevant definition or rule, then work in a logical order.

*Solve.* Every suitable position lies on the perpendicular bisector of $AB$; a point may then be chosen to meet terrain constraints.

*Look back.* Compare the result with the data and use an independent check.

Therefore, perpendicular bisector of $AB$.
:::

## Common mistakes and connections

:::warning What can go wrong?
- **Confusing base and legs.** In a triangle isosceles at $A$, calling $AB$ the base. Vertex $A$ joins equal sides $AB,AC$; the opposite base is $BC$. Locate the vertex first, then choose the opposite side as base.
- **Omitting a perpendicular-bisector condition.** Treating every line perpendicular to $AB$ as its perpendicular bisector. The line must also pass through the midpoint of $AB$. Check both perpendicularity and bisection.
:::

**Connections.** Isosceles triangles link equal sides to equal angles; perpendicular bisectors link position to equal distance. They meet in symmetry, circle construction and fair-location problems. ## Summary

- An isosceles triangle has equal legs and equal base angles.
- Equal angles imply equal opposite sides.
- A perpendicular bisector is perpendicular at the segment midpoint.
- A point on the perpendicular bisector is equidistant from the endpoints.
- In an isosceles triangle, the vertex altitude is also a median and angle bisector.
