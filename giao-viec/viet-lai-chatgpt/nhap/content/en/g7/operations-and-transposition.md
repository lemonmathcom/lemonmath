Two students evaluate $18-3\cdot4+2$ differently because one subtracts first and the other multiplies first. A common order gives one value; in an equation, which transformation is really behind moving a term across the equals sign?

## order of operations, brackets and transposition in equations

**A short exploration.**
By the agreed order, $18-3\cdot4+2=18-12+2=8$. For $x+5=12$, subtract $5$ from both sides to get $x+5-5=12-5$, so $x=7$. Saying “move $5$ across and change its sign” abbreviates the same operation on both sides.

:::definition Order of operations
Work inside brackets first, then powers, then multiplication and division from left to right, and finally addition and subtraction from left to right. A fraction bar also groups the whole numerator and denominator.
:::

:::definition Transposition
Adding or subtracting the same number on both sides preserves an equality. Therefore, when a term is transposed from one side to the other, its sign changes.
:::

**Why does this work?**
The order of operations is a language convention, like punctuation in writing. Transposition is not magic: both sides remain equal because the same operation is applied. Writing an intermediate step prevents sign errors and allows substitution to check a solution.

**How to check your own work.** For a question on this topic, first state in words what is given and what must be found. Next write the rule you will use, substitute values or reason one step at a time, and then check the result in a different way: put it back into the data, estimate its size, or compare it with a diagram or number line. If two methods disagree, locate the first step where they differ instead of changing only the final answer. This habit catches sign errors, missing units and overlooked cases.

**Systems view.** order of operations, brackets and transposition in equations is not an isolated trick. Follow the chain: the data determine a model, the model determines the calculation, and the result must return to the original situation. When one piece of data changes, predict whether the result should increase, decrease or stay unchanged before calculating again.

## Worked examples and applications

:::example Multiplication before addition
Calculate $7+3\cdot5$.

**Solution.**
Multiply first: $7+15=22$.

Therefore, $22$.
:::

:::example Brackets change the order
Calculate $(7+3)\cdot5$.

**Solution.**
Work inside brackets first: $10\cdot5=50$.

Therefore, $50$.
:::

:::example Several operation levels
Calculate $2^3+24\div6-5$.

**Solution.**
$2^3=8$, $24\div6=4$, then $8+4-5=7$.

Therefore, $7$.
:::

:::example A remaining budget
A group has $500$ thousand dong, buys $4$ material sets at $65$ thousand dong each and pays $40$ thousand dong delivery. Find the amount left.

**Solution.**

*Understand.* Record the given data, the required result and any conditions.

*Plan.* Choose the relevant definition or rule, then work in a logical order.

*Solve.* The cost is $4\cdot65+40=300$; the amount left is $500-300=200$ thousand dong.

*Look back.* Compare the result with the data and use an independent check.

Therefore, $200$ thousand dong.
:::

## Common mistakes and connections

:::warning What can go wrong?
- **Working entirely left to right.** Evaluating $2+3\cdot4$ as $5\cdot4=20$. Multiplication has priority over addition. Calculate $3\cdot4=12$, then $2+12=14$.
- **Changing a sign without transposing.** In $x-5=8$, changing $-5$ to $+5$ while leaving it on the left. The sign change results from adding $5$ to both sides. Write $x=8+5=13$ or show addition on both sides.
:::

**Connections.** Order of operations makes expressions an unambiguous language. Transposition begins equation manipulation, but the deeper principle is always preserving equality through balanced operations. When solving a problem, separate three layers: the data, the mathematical relationship and the conclusion. Before substituting values, state why the chosen rule applies; after each step, check signs, units and conditions. A diagram, small table or simple case often reveals a missing assumption. Estimate before calculating so that you know whether the answer should be large or small, positive or negative. After obtaining a result, use an independent check: substitute it back into the data, measure it on the diagram, or test a boundary case. If one item of data changes, predict which part of the method changes and which part remains valid. This approach turns a formula into a connected system of reasons and makes the idea usable in an unfamiliar situation instead of only in a memorised exercise. It also produces written work that another student can follow, question and verify line by line.

## Summary

- Work with brackets and powers before multiplication and division, then addition and subtraction.
- Operations of equal priority are performed from left to right.
- A fraction bar groups both numerator and denominator.
- Transposing a term changes its sign.
- Substitute a solution into the equation to check it.
