A hypothetical mixture uses $3$ parts water for $2$ parts solution. With $8$ parts solution, how much water keeps the same mixture? The equality $\dfrac32=\dfrac{x}{8}$ expresses two equal ratios.

## proportions, their properties and applications

**A short exploration.**
Cross multiplication in $\dfrac32=\dfrac{x}{8}$ gives $3\cdot8=2x$, so $x=12$. Check: $12:8=3:2$. Equality of the extreme and mean products characterises a proportion when denominators differ from $0$.

:::definition Proportion
A proportion is an equality of two ratios: $\dfrac ab=\dfrac cd$, with $b,d\ne0$. Numbers $a,d$ are the extremes; $b,c$ are the means.
:::

:::definition Cross-product property
$\dfrac ab=\dfrac cd$ if and only if $ad=bc$, provided denominators differ from $0$. This property checks a proportion and finds an unknown term.
:::

**Why does this work?**
Multiplying $\dfrac ab=\dfrac cd$ by $bd$ different from $0$ gives $ad=bc$. Conversely, dividing $ad=bc$ by $bd$ restores the ratio equality. Cross multiplication is an equality transformation, not an isolated trick.

**How to check your own work.** For a question on this topic, first state in words what is given and what must be found. Next write the rule you will use, substitute values or reason one step at a time, and then check the result in a different way: put it back into the data, estimate its size, or compare it with a diagram or number line. If two methods disagree, locate the first step where they differ instead of changing only the final answer. This habit catches sign errors, missing units and overlooked cases.

**Systems view.** proportions, their properties and applications is not an isolated trick. Follow the chain: the data determine a model, the model determines the calculation, and the result must return to the original situation. When one piece of data changes, predict whether the result should increase, decrease or stay unchanged before calculating again.

## Worked examples and applications

:::example Checking a proportion
Check $\dfrac6{15}=\dfrac{14}{35}$.

**Solution.**
$6\cdot35=210$ and $15\cdot14=210$, so it is a proportion.

Therefore, true.
:::

:::example Finding an unknown
Solve $\dfrac{x}{12}=\dfrac58$.

**Solution.**
$8x=12\cdot5=60$, so $x=\dfrac{60}{8}=7.5$.

Therefore, $7.5$.
:::

:::example Finding an unknown denominator
Solve $\dfrac7x=\dfrac{21}{18}$.

**Solution.**
$7\cdot18=21x$, so $126=21x$ and $x=6$.

Therefore, $6$.
:::

:::example A scale map
On a map, $4$ cm represents $10$ km. Two points are $7$ cm apart on the map. Find the real distance.

**Solution.**

*Understand.* Record the given data, the required result and any conditions.

*Plan.* Choose the relevant definition or rule, then work in a logical order.

*Solve.* $\dfrac4{10}=\dfrac7x$, so $4x=70$ and $x=17.5$ km.

*Look back.* Compare the result with the data and use an independent check.

Therefore, $17.5$ km.
:::

## Common mistakes and connections

:::warning What can go wrong?
- **Mismatching quantity order.** Writing cm:km on one side but km:cm on the other. The ratios then compare different relationships. Label numerator and denominator quantities in the same order.
- **Cross multiplying with a zero denominator.** Ignoring the condition $b,d\ne0$. A ratio with denominator $0$ is undefined. State denominator restrictions before transforming.
:::

**Connections.** Proportions preserve relative relationships in scaling, mixtures and unit conversion. They lead directly to direct proportion and fractional equations. When solving a problem, separate three layers: the data, the mathematical relationship and the conclusion. Before substituting values, state why the chosen rule applies; after each step, check signs, units and conditions. A diagram, small table or simple case often reveals a missing assumption. Estimate before calculating so that you know whether the answer should be large or small, positive or negative. After obtaining a result, use an independent check: substitute it back into the data, measure it on the diagram, or test a boundary case. If one item of data changes, predict which part of the method changes and which part remains valid. This approach turns a formula into a connected system of reasons and makes the idea usable in an unfamiliar situation instead of only in a memorised exercise. It also produces written work that another student can follow, question and verify line by line.

## Summary

- $\dfrac ab=\dfrac cd$ is a proportion with denominators different from $0$.
- A proportion is equivalent to $ad=bc$.
- Keep quantity order consistent in both ratios.
- Check that a solution does not make a denominator $0$.
- Check by simplification or cross products.
