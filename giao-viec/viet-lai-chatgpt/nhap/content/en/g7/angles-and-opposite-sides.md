In a triangle, the greatest angle is opposite the longest side. With side lengths $5,7,9$ cm, the angle order can be found without measuring. Why do an angle and its opposite side always change in the same direction?

## comparing angles and opposite sides in a triangle

**A short exploration.**
Draw two triangles with two fixed sides but increase the included angle. As the sides rotate apart, the distance between their free endpoints increases; that distance is the opposite side. This suggests that a larger angle faces a longer side, and the converse also holds.

:::definition Comparison theorem
In a triangle, the larger angle is opposite the longer side. Conversely, the longer side is opposite the larger angle. Equal angles face equal sides and conversely.
:::

:::definition Triangle inequality
The sum of any two side lengths of a triangle is greater than the remaining side. This ensures the three segments can meet to form a triangle.
:::

**Why does this work?**
If the side opposite a greater angle were not longer, congruence or an auxiliary-point comparison would contradict the angle opening. The hinge model also shows that fixed arms have endpoints farther apart as their included angle increases. Triangle inequality follows because a straight segment is the shortest path between two points.

If two methods disagree, locate the first step where they differ instead of changing only the final answer. This habit catches sign errors, missing units and overlooked cases.

**Systems view.** comparing angles and opposite sides in a triangle is not an isolated trick. Follow the chain: the data determine a model, the model determines the calculation, and the result must return to the original situation. When one piece of data changes, predict whether the result should increase, decrease or stay unchanged before calculating again.

## Worked examples and applications

:::example Ordering angles from sides
In $\triangle ABC$, $AB=5$, $BC=7$, $CA=9$. Order $\angle A,\angle B,\angle C$ increasingly.

**Solution.**
$AB$ faces $\angle C$, $BC$ faces $\angle A$, $CA$ faces $\angle B$. Since $5<7<9$, $\angle C<\angle A<\angle B$.

Therefore, $\angle C<\angle A<\angle B$.
:::

:::example Ordering sides from angles
In a triangle, $\angle P=40^\circ$, $\angle Q=65^\circ$, $\angle R=75^\circ$. Order the sides.

**Solution.**
The opposite sides are $QR,PR,PQ$. Since $40<65<75$, $QR<PR<PQ$.

Therefore, $QR<PR<PQ$.
:::

:::example Finding the longest side
A triangle has angles $35^\circ,55^\circ,90^\circ$. Which side is longest?

**Solution.**
The side opposite $90^\circ$ is longest; in a right triangle it is the hypotenuse.

Therefore, hypotenuse.
:::

:::example Choosing a direct route
Points $A,B,C$ form a triangle. Compare direct route $AC$ with route $AB+BC$.

**Solution.**

*Understand.* Record the given data, the required result and any conditions.

*Plan.* Choose the relevant definition or rule, then work in a logical order.

*Solve.* Triangle inequality gives $AC<AB+BC$, so the direct route is shorter.

*Look back.* Compare the result with the data and use an independent check.

Therefore, $AC<AB+BC$.
:::

## Common mistakes and connections

:::warning What can go wrong?
- **Matching the wrong opposite side.** Calling side $AB$ opposite $\angle A$. $AB$ touches vertex $A$; the side opposite $A$ is $BC$. Circle the vertex and choose the side not touching it.
- **Checking an arbitrary inequality only.** Checking two large sides rather than the two shorter against the longest. The decisive condition is that the shorter sides exceed the longest. Order the lengths, then compare the shorter-side sum with the longest.
:::

**Connections.** The side–angle relationship converts length information into directional information and conversely. It supports triangle classification, route optimisation and many geometric inequalities. When solving a problem, separate three layers: the data, the mathematical relationship and the conclusion. Before substituting values, state why the chosen rule applies; after each step, check signs, units and conditions. A diagram, small table or simple case often reveals a missing assumption. Estimate before calculating so that you know whether the answer should be large or small, positive or negative. After obtaining a result, use an independent check: substitute it back into the data, measure it on the diagram, or test a boundary case. If one item of data changes, predict which part of the method changes and which part remains valid. This approach turns a formula into a connected system of reasons and makes the idea usable in an unfamiliar situation instead of only in a memorised exercise. It also produces written work that another student can follow, question and verify line by line.

## Summary

- A greater angle faces a longer side.
- A longer side faces a greater angle.
- Equal angles face equal sides and conversely.
- Any two side lengths sum to more than the third.
- To find a side opposite a vertex, choose the side not touching it.
