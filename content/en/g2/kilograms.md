Mai carries a bag of rice and Nam carries a bag of cotton wool. Nam says his bag is bigger so it must be heavier, but Mai feels that the rice is heavier.

How can you tell which bag is heavier, and by how much? And how can you tell how much water a bottle or a jerry can holds?

## Kilograms

To find out which object is heavier, use **scales**. With **balance scales**, you put one object on each pan. The pan that **goes lower** holds the **heavier** object. If the two pans stay level, the two objects have the same mass.

To find out how heavy an object is, use **scales with a dial or marks**. The number the pointer shows is the mass of the object.

:::definition The kilogram
The **kilogram** is a unit for measuring **mass** (how heavy an object is). The kilogram is written **kg**.
:::

Measurements in kg can be added and subtracted like ordinary numbers, and then you write the unit kg in the answer. For example, $5\ \text{kg}+4\ \text{kg}=9\ \text{kg}$. Why? Adding measurements in the same unit means putting together parts of the same kind.

:::example Reading the scales
A bag of rice is placed on a set of scales. The pointer is exactly at the mark labelled 3 (as in the picture above). What is the mass of the bag of rice in kilograms?

**Solution.** The pointer is at the mark labelled 3, so the bag of rice has a mass of 3 kg.
:::

:::example Balance scales
On a set of balance scales, the pan with the melon is lower than the pan with the orange. Which is heavier?

**Solution.** The heavier pan goes lower. The pan with the melon is lower, so the melon is heavier than the orange, and the orange is lighter than the melon.
:::

:::example Calculating with kilograms
Work out: a) $8\ \text{kg}+7\ \text{kg}$; b) $17\ \text{kg}-9\ \text{kg}$.

**Solution.** Add or subtract the numbers, then write the unit kg in the answer:
$$8\ \text{kg}+7\ \text{kg}=15\ \text{kg},$$
$$17\ \text{kg}-9\ \text{kg}=8\ \text{kg}.$$
So a) $15$ kg; b) $8$ kg.
:::

## Litres

Water, milk and cooking oil are liquids. To find out how much liquid a bottle or a can holds, use the **litre**.

:::definition The litre
The **litre** is a unit for measuring the amount of a liquid (water, milk, cooking oil, …). The litre is written **l**.
:::

You measure out water with a cup, a jug or a bottle with marks on it. Measurements in l can also be added and subtracted like ordinary numbers. For example, $6\ \text{l}+5\ \text{l}=11\ \text{l}$. Remember: kg measures how **heavy** something is, and l measures the amount of liquid something **holds**.

:::example Two cans of oil
Suppose the first can holds 7 litres of oil and the second can holds 5 litres of oil. How many litres of oil do the two cans hold altogether?

**Solution.** Add the number of litres in the two cans:

Solution

The number of litres of oil in the two cans altogether is:
$$7+5=12\ \text{(l)}.$$
Answer: 12 l of oil.
:::

:::example The water tank (Apply it)
Suppose a tank holds 18 litres of water. Mum takes out 9 litres to water the plants. How many litres of water are left in the tank?

**Understand.** We know the tank holds 18 litres and Mum takes out 9 litres. We are asked how many litres are left.

**Plan.** "Takes out" means taking away, so we subtract $18-9$.

**Solve.**

Solution

The number of litres of water left in the tank is:
$$18-9=9\ \text{(l)}.$$
Answer: 9 l of water.

**Look back.** Check with addition: $9+9=18$, which is correct. What if Mum then pours 6 more litres into the tank? How many litres does it hold then? We work out $9+6=15$, which is 15 l.
:::

:::warning Mistakes to watch out for
- **Using the wrong unit.** Wrong: "The bag of rice has a mass of 5 l". Why it is wrong: how heavy something is is measured in kg, while l is for measuring out liquids. Right: "The bag of rice has a mass of 5 kg".
- **Forgetting the unit in a calculation.** Wrong: $5\ \text{kg}+4\ \text{kg}=9$. Why it is wrong: the result of a measurement needs its unit. Right: $5\ \text{kg}+4\ \text{kg}=9\ \text{kg}$.
- **Misreading the balance.** Wrong: the right pan is higher, so the object on it is heavier. Why it is wrong: the heavier pan is pulled down, and the higher pan is the lighter one. Right: the object on the lower pan is the heavier one.
:::

## Summary

- The kilogram (kg) is a unit of mass. On balance scales, the lower pan holds the heavier object. On scales with marks, the number the pointer shows is the mass in kilograms.
- The litre (l) is a unit for measuring out liquids.
- Measurements in the same unit are added and subtracted like ordinary numbers, and then you write the unit in the answer.
- Remember to write the unit kg or l in the answer.

Solution

Suppose the first tank holds 12 litres of water. The second tank holds 5 litres more than the first. How many litres of water does the second tank hold?

::hint
**What you need:** "more than" means add.

1. What do we know? What are we asked?
2. Does the second tank hold more or less than the first? Which calculation do you use?

::solution
Solution

The number of litres of water the second tank holds is:
$$12+5=17\ \text{(l)}.$$

:::exercise
Remember the lesson "How many more or fewer?". A sack of rice has a mass of 15 kg and a sack of maize has a mass of 9 kg. Which is heavier? By how many kilograms?

::hint
**What you need:** to find how many more or fewer, subtract the smaller number from the larger.

**Steps:**
1. Compare 15 and 9.
2. Work out the difference and write the unit kg.

::solution
We have $15>9$, so the sack of rice is heavier. It is heavier than the sack of maize by:
$$15-9=6\ \text{(kg)}.$$

**Answer.** The sack of rice is heavier, by 6 kg.
:::

:::exercise Challenge
Suppose a can holds 10 litres of oil. The first time, 4 litres are poured out, and the second time, 3 more litres are poured out. How many litres of oil are left in the can?

::hint
**What you need:** "poured out" means taking away: use subtraction.

**Steps:**
1. How many litres are left after the first time?
2. How many litres are left after the second time?

::solution
After the first time, the can holds:
$$10-4=6\ \text{(l)}.$$
After the second time, the can holds:
$$6-3=3\ \text{(l)}.$$

**Check.** The total poured out is $4+3=7$ and $10-7=3$, which is correct.

**Answer.** 3 l of oil.
:::
