:::goals
- Write lengths, masses and areas given in two units as decimals.
- Change a measurement written as a decimal into a smaller unit.
- Round a decimal to the nearest whole number, to one decimal place and to two decimal places.
- Solve real-life problems with measurements written as decimals.

Mr Tu weighs a bag of mangoes and the digital scale shows $2.347$ kg. When he sells it he says, "This bag is about $2.3$ kilograms."

How many kilograms and how many grams is $2.347$ kg? And why does he say "about $2.3$ kg"? This lesson answers both questions.

## Writing measurements as decimals

### Let's explore

Let us write $2$ m $35$ cm as a measurement in one unit. Since $1$ cm is $\dfrac{1}{100}$ of a metre, $35$ cm is $\dfrac{35}{100}$ m, which is $0.35$ m. So $2$ m $35$ cm $=2.35$ m.

Do the same for Mr Tu's mangoes. Since $1$ g is $\dfrac{1}{1000}$ of a kilogram, $347$ g is $\dfrac{347}{1000}$ kg, which is $0.347$ kg. So $2.347$ kg is $2$ kg $347$ g.

:::theorem Writing a measurement with two units as a decimal
To write a measurement with two neighbouring units as a decimal:
1. Write the part in the smaller unit as a decimal fraction of the larger unit.
2. Write that fraction as a decimal, then join it to the part in the larger unit.

The number of digits in the decimal part depends on how many times apart the two units are: $10$ times means one digit ($1\ \text{dm}=0.1\ \text{m}$); $100$ times means two digits ($1\ \text{cm}=0.01\ \text{m}$, $1\ \text{dm}^2=0.01\ \text{m}^2$); $1000$ times means three digits ($1\ \text{g}=0.001\ \text{kg}$, $1\ \text{kg}=0.001$ tonne).
:::

If the part in the smaller unit does not have enough digits, write a $0$ at the start of the decimal part, for example $7$ cm $=0.07$ m.

**Connection.** This lesson uses the table of units from the review lesson and the way of writing decimals you have just learnt. In later lessons you will calculate with measurements like these.

### Examples

We start with lengths.

:::example Write lengths as decimals
a) $3$ m $25$ cm $=?$ m; b) $45$ cm $=?$ m; c) $5$ dm $=?$ m; d) $1$ m $7$ cm $=?$ m.

**Solution.** a) $25$ cm $=\dfrac{25}{100}$ m $=0.25$ m, so $3$ m $25$ cm $=3.25$ m.

b) $45$ cm $=\dfrac{45}{100}$ m $=0.45$ m.

c) $5$ dm $=\dfrac{5}{10}$ m $=0.5$ m.

d) $7$ cm $=\dfrac{7}{100}$ m $=0.07$ m, so $1$ m $7$ cm $=1.07$ m.

So a) $3.25$ m; b) $0.45$ m; c) $0.5$ m; d) $1.07$ m.
:::

Now masses, where the two units are $1000$ times apart.

:::example Write masses as decimals
a) $2$ kg $350$ g $=?$ kg; b) $750$ g $=?$ kg; c) $2$ kg $50$ g $=?$ kg; d) $3$ tonnes $450$ kg $=?$ tonnes.

**Solution.** a) $350$ g $=\dfrac{350}{1000}$ kg $=0.35$ kg, so $2$ kg $350$ g $=2.35$ kg.

b) $750$ g $=\dfrac{750}{1000}$ kg $=0.75$ kg.

c) $50$ g $=\dfrac{50}{1000}$ kg $=0.05$ kg, so $2$ kg $50$ g $=2.05$ kg.

d) $450$ kg $=\dfrac{450}{1000}$ tonne $=0.45$ tonne, so $3$ tonnes $450$ kg $=3.45$ tonnes.

So a) $2.35$ kg; b) $0.75$ kg; c) $2.05$ kg; d) $3.45$ tonnes.
:::

For areas, neighbouring units are $100$ times apart, and you can also work backwards.

:::example Measurements of area
a) Write $3\ \text{m}^2\ 25\ \text{dm}^2$ as a decimal in $\text{m}^2$. b) Write $2.5\ \text{m}^2$ as a measurement with two units.

**Solution.** a) $25\ \text{dm}^2=\dfrac{25}{100}\ \text{m}^2=0.25\ \text{m}^2$, so $3\ \text{m}^2\ 25\ \text{dm}^2=3.25\ \text{m}^2$.

b) We have $2.5=2+\dfrac{5}{10}=2+\dfrac{50}{100}$, so $2.5\ \text{m}^2=2\ \text{m}^2\ 50\ \text{dm}^2$.

So a) $3.25\ \text{m}^2$; b) $2\ \text{m}^2\ 50\ \text{dm}^2$.
:::

You can use decimals to compare measurements in different units.

:::example Two bags of mangoes
Suppose the first bag of mangoes has mass $2.35$ kg and the second bag has mass $2$ kg $400$ g. Which bag is heavier?

**Solution.** Write the mass of the second bag as a decimal: $400$ g $=0.4$ kg, so $2$ kg $400$ g $=2.4$ kg.

Write $2.4=2.40$. Since $35<40$, we have $2.35<2.40$.

So the second bag is heavier.
:::

:::warning Mistakes to watch out for
- **Leaving out the digit $0$ at the start of the decimal part.** Wrong: $1$ m $7$ cm $=1.7$ m. Why it is wrong: $7$ cm $=\dfrac{7}{100}$ m $=0.07$ m, not $0.7$ m. Right: $1.07$ m.
- **Changing back with the wrong place.** Wrong: $2.5\ \text{m}^2=2\ \text{m}^2\ 5\ \text{dm}^2$. Why it is wrong: each step of area units has two digits, so $0.5\ \text{m}^2=0.50\ \text{m}^2=50\ \text{dm}^2$. Right: $2\ \text{m}^2\ 50\ \text{dm}^2$.
:::

## Rounding decimals

### Let's explore

The number $2.347$ lies between $2.3$ and $2.4$. The number exactly halfway between them is $2.35$. Since $2.347<2.35$, the number $2.347$ is closer to $2.3$. That is why Mr Tu says "about $2.3$ kg".

:::graph On the number line: 2.347 is closer to 2.3 than to 2.4
alt: A number line from 2.25 to 2.45 with the points 2.3, 2.347 and 2.4 marked and a dashed line marking the midpoint 2.35
x: 2.25..2.45
y: -1..1
segment: (2.3, 0) (2.4, 0)
point: (2.3, 0) "2.3" below
point: (2.347, 0) "2.347" above
point: (2.4, 0) "2.4" below
vline: 2.35 ; dashed
:::

:::theorem The rule for rounding decimals
To round a decimal to a given place, look at the digit **immediately to the right** of that place:
- If that digit is **less than $5$**, keep the digit in the rounding place and drop all the digits to its right.
- If that digit is **greater than or equal to $5$**, increase the digit in the rounding place by $1$ and drop all the digits to its right.

Rounding to the nearest whole number means rounding to the ones place.
:::

The rule works because we pick the nearest round number: a digit to the right that is less than $5$ means the leftover part is less than half a unit of the rounding place.

### Examples

:::example Rounding to the nearest whole number
Round $7.46$, $12.5$ and $0.89$ to the nearest whole number.

**Solution.** We look at the digit in the tenths place.

- $7.46$ has tenths digit $4<5$, so it rounds to $7$.
- $12.5$ has tenths digit $5$, so it rounds to $13$.
- $0.89$ has tenths digit $8>5$, so it rounds to $1$.

So $7.46\approx7$; $12.5\approx13$; $0.89\approx1$.
:::

Next, rounding to one and two decimal places, including a case where we carry into the place before.

:::example Rounding to one and two decimal places
Round $2.347$ and $5.962$ to one decimal place and to two decimal places.

**Solution.** For $2.347$: the hundredths digit is $4<5$, so rounding to one decimal place gives $2.3$. The thousandths digit is $7>5$, so rounding to two decimal places gives $2.35$.

For $5.962$: the hundredths digit is $6>5$, so rounding to one decimal place gives $5.9+0.1=6.0$. The thousandths digit is $2<5$, so rounding to two decimal places gives $5.96$.

So $2.347\approx2.3\approx2.35$ (to one and two decimal places) and $5.962\approx6.0$; $5.96$.
:::

Finally, Mr Tu's problem, solved in four steps.

:::example Mr Tu's bag of mangoes
The digital scale shows $2.347$ kg. Mr Tu writes the mass rounded to one decimal place. What mass does he write, and how many grams is the written value away from the scale reading?

**Understand.** The scale reading is $2.347$ kg. We need to round it to one decimal place, then compare with the original.

**Plan.** Use the rounding rule, then change to grams to find the difference of the two measurements.

**Solve.** The hundredths digit of $2.347$ is $4<5$, so rounding to one decimal place gives $2.3$ kg. Change to grams: $2.347\ \text{kg}=2347$ g and $2.3\ \text{kg}=2300$ g, so the difference of the two measurements is $2347-2300=47$ g.

So he writes $2.3$ kg, which is $47$ g away from the reading.

**Look back.** Is there another way? Yes: subtract directly, $2.347-2.3=0.047$ kg, which is $47$ g. Is it reasonable? Yes, because rounding to one decimal place changes the number by at most half a unit of that place, that is at most $0.05$ kg $=50$ g, and $47<50$.
:::

:::warning Mistakes to watch out for
- **Dropping digits without checking.** Wrong: rounding $2.347$ to two decimal places gives $2.34$. Why it is wrong: the digit immediately to the right of the hundredths place is $7\ge5$, so we must add $1$. Right: $2.35$.
- **Rounding more than once.** Wrong: rounding $2.347$ to one decimal place by looking at the digit $7$ gives $2.4$. Why it is wrong: we only look at the digit immediately to the right of the rounding place, which is $4<5$. Right: $2.3$.
:::

## Summary

- Write the part in the smaller unit as a decimal fraction of the larger unit, then as a decimal; add a digit $0$ when digits are missing.
- The number of decimal places depends on whether the two units are $10$, $100$ or $1000$ times apart.
- Rounding: look at the digit immediately to the right of the rounding place; less than $5$ means keep, $5$ or more means add $1$.
- When you round to a place, keep only the digits up to that place.
- To compare measurements in different units, write them in the same unit as decimals.

:::exercise
Write these measurements as decimals in metres: a) $4$ m $8$ dm; b) $6$ m $25$ cm; c) $85$ cm; d) $3$ m $4$ cm.

::hint
**What you need:** $1\ \text{dm}=0.1\ \text{m}$ and $1\ \text{cm}=0.01\ \text{m}$.

**Steps:**
1. Write the part in the smaller unit as a decimal fraction of a metre.
2. Write it as a decimal, adding a digit $0$ if needed.

::solution
a) $8$ dm $=0.8$ m, so $4$ m $8$ dm $=4.8$ m.

b) $25$ cm $=0.25$ m, so $6$ m $25$ cm $=6.25$ m.

c) $85$ cm $=0.85$ m.

d) $4$ cm $=0.04$ m, so $3$ m $4$ cm $=3.04$ m.

**Answer.** a) $4.8$ m; b) $6.25$ m; c) $0.85$ m; d) $3.04$ m.
:::

:::exercise
Write these measurements as decimals in kilograms: a) $3$ kg $500$ g; b) $2$ kg $75$ g; c) $400$ g; d) $1$ kg $8$ g.

::hint
**What you need:** $1\ \text{g}=0.001\ \text{kg}$, so the decimal part has three digits.

**Steps:**
1. Write the number of grams as a fraction with denominator $1000$.
2. Write it as a decimal with three digits, then remove any digits $0$ at the end.

::solution
a) $500$ g $=0.500$ kg $=0.5$ kg, so $3$ kg $500$ g $=3.5$ kg.

b) $75$ g $=0.075$ kg, so $2$ kg $75$ g $=2.075$ kg.

c) $400$ g $=0.400$ kg $=0.4$ kg.

d) $8$ g $=0.008$ kg, so $1$ kg $8$ g $=1.008$ kg.

**Answer.** a) $3.5$ kg; b) $2.075$ kg; c) $0.4$ kg; d) $1.008$ kg.
:::

:::exercise
Change to a smaller unit: a) $2.5$ km $=?$ m; b) $0.75$ kg $=?$ g; c) $3.2$ tonnes $=?$ kg; d) $4.05$ m $=?$ cm.

::hint
**What you need:** separate the whole-number part and the decimal part, and change each part.

**Steps:**
1. Write the decimal part as a decimal fraction of the larger unit.
2. Change each part into the smaller unit, then add.

::solution
a) $2.5\ \text{km}=2\ \text{km}+\dfrac{500}{1000}\ \text{km}=2000+500=2500$ m.

b) $0.75\ \text{kg}=\dfrac{750}{1000}\ \text{kg}=750$ g.

c) $3.2$ tonnes $=3$ tonnes $+\dfrac{200}{1000}$ tonne $=3000+200=3200$ kg.

d) $4.05\ \text{m}=4\ \text{m}+\dfrac{5}{100}\ \text{m}=400+5=405$ cm.

**Answer.** a) $2500$ m; b) $750$ g; c) $3200$ kg; d) $405$ cm.
:::

:::exercise
Write these measurements of area: a) $7\ \text{m}^2\ 40\ \text{dm}^2$ as a decimal in $\text{m}^2$; b) $2\ \text{m}^2\ 5\ \text{dm}^2$ as a decimal in $\text{m}^2$; c) $6.3\ \text{m}^2$ as a measurement with two units.

::hint
**What you need:** $1\ \text{dm}^2=0.01\ \text{m}^2$, so each step of area has two decimal places.

**Steps:**
1. Write the number of $\text{dm}^2$ as a fraction with denominator $100$.
2. For part c, write $0.3=0.30$.

::solution
a) $40\ \text{dm}^2=0.4\ \text{m}^2$, so $7\ \text{m}^2\ 40\ \text{dm}^2=7.4\ \text{m}^2$.

b) $5\ \text{dm}^2=0.05\ \text{m}^2$, so $2\ \text{m}^2\ 5\ \text{dm}^2=2.05\ \text{m}^2$.

c) $6.3=6+0.30$, so $6.3\ \text{m}^2=6\ \text{m}^2\ 30\ \text{dm}^2$.

**Answer.** a) $7.4\ \text{m}^2$; b) $2.05\ \text{m}^2$; c) $6\ \text{m}^2\ 30\ \text{dm}^2$.
:::

:::exercise
Write $>$, $<$ or $=$ in the gap: a) $4\ \text{kg}\ 600\ \text{g}\ \ldots\ 4.06\ \text{kg}$; b) $2\ \text{m}\ 30\ \text{cm}\ \ldots\ 2.3\ \text{m}$; c) $750\ \text{g}\ \ldots\ 0.8\ \text{kg}$.

::hint
**What you need:** write both measurements in the same unit, then compare the two decimals.

**Steps:**
1. Write the left-hand side as a decimal.
2. Compare the two decimals.

::solution
a) $4\ \text{kg}\ 600\ \text{g}=4.6\ \text{kg}$. Since $4.6>4.06$, write $>$.

b) $2\ \text{m}\ 30\ \text{cm}=2.3\ \text{m}$, so write $=$.

c) $750\ \text{g}=0.75\ \text{kg}$. Since $0.75<0.80$, write $<$.

**Answer.** a) $>$; b) $=$; c) $<$.
:::

:::exercise
Round to the nearest whole number: a) $6.49$; b) $15.5$; c) $0.6$; d) $99.5$.

::hint
**What you need:** look at the tenths digit: less than $5$ means keep the whole-number part, $5$ or more means add $1$ to it.

**Steps:**
1. Find the tenths digit.
2. Apply the rounding rule.

::solution
a) The tenths digit is $4<5$, so $6.49\approx6$.

b) The tenths digit is $5$, so $15.5\approx16$.

c) The tenths digit is $6>5$, so $0.6\approx1$.

d) The tenths digit is $5$, so $99.5\approx100$.

**Answer.** a) $6$; b) $16$; c) $1$; d) $100$.
:::

:::exercise
Round to one decimal place: a) $3.14$; b) $8.96$; c) $0.449$.

::hint
**What you need:** look at the digit immediately to the right of the tenths place, which is the hundredths digit.

**Steps:**
1. Find the hundredths digit.
2. Keep the tenths digit or add $1$ to it (carry when the digit is $9$).

::solution
a) The hundredths digit is $4<5$, so $3.14\approx3.1$.

b) The hundredths digit is $6>5$, so $8.9+0.1=9.0$, that is, $8.96\approx9.0$.

c) The hundredths digit is $4<5$, so $0.449\approx0.4$.

**Answer.** a) $3.1$; b) $9.0$; c) $0.4$.
:::

:::exercise
Suppose a digital scale shows $12.348$ kg. Round this mass to one decimal place and to two decimal places.

::hint
**What you need:** to round to a place, look at the digit immediately to the right of that place.

**Steps:**
1. What do we know? What is asked?
2. Round each time starting from the original $12.348$, not from the previous result.

::solution
To one decimal place: the hundredths digit is $4<5$, so $12.348\approx12.3$ kg.

To two decimal places: the thousandths digit is $8>5$, so $12.348\approx12.35$ kg.

**Answer.** $12.3$ kg and $12.35$ kg.
:::

:::exercise
Remember the lesson on comparing decimals. In a long jump competition, Mai jumps $2.15$ m, Lan jumps $2$ m $18$ cm and Ha jumps $205$ cm. Who jumps the furthest? Put the friends in order from the furthest to the shortest.

::hint
**What you need:** write all three measurements in metres, then compare the decimals.

**Steps:**
1. Write $2$ m $18$ cm and $205$ cm as decimals in metres.
2. Compare the tenths, then the hundredths.

::solution
We have $2$ m $18$ cm $=2.18$ m and $205$ cm $=2$ m $5$ cm $=2.05$ m. The three measurements are $2.15$ m; $2.18$ m; $2.05$ m.

Since $2.18>2.15>2.05$, Lan jumps the furthest, then Mai, then Ha.

**Answer.** Lan jumps the furthest; order: Lan, Mai, Ha.
:::

:::exercise Challenge
A decimal with two digits in the decimal part rounds to $4.5$ when rounded to one decimal place. Find the least and the greatest number it could be.

::hint
**What you need:** a number that rounds up to $4.5$ has tenths digit $4$ and hundredths digit $5$ or more, or has tenths digit $5$ and hundredths digit less than $5$.

**Steps:**
1. The least number has tenths digit $4$ and the smallest hundredths digit that rounds up.
2. The greatest number has tenths digit $5$ and the largest hundredths digit that rounds down.

::solution
The least number has the form $4.4a$ with $a\ge5$, so it is $4.45$. The greatest number has the form $4.5b$ with $b<5$, so it is $4.54$.

Check: $4.45\approx4.5$ (the hundredths digit is $5$) and $4.54\approx4.5$ (the hundredths digit is $4$).

**Answer.** The least is $4.45$; the greatest is $4.54$.
:::
