:::goals
- Recognise one-$n$th of a shape, and read and write $\dfrac{1}{n}$.
- Know that the more equal parts there are, the smaller each part is.
- Find one-$n$th of a number by dividing, and solve word problems.
:::

Mum cuts a cake into equal pieces to share among $4$ friends, one piece each. What fraction of the cake does each friend get?

You could answer in words, but maths has a short way to write "one out of so many". This lesson shows you how to read it, write it and use it.

## Let's explore

We think of the cake as a rectangle split into $4$ equal parts.

The shape is split into $4$ **equal** parts. Each part is **one quarter** of the shape, written $\dfrac{1}{4}$. So each friend gets $\dfrac{1}{4}$ of the cake.

If we split into $2$ equal parts, each part is $\dfrac{1}{2}$ of the shape. If we split into $8$ equal parts, each part is $\dfrac{1}{8}$. The more equal parts we make, the smaller each part is.

:::theorem Unit fractions
Split a shape (or a number) into $n$ equal parts. Each part is **one-$n$th** of the shape (of the number), written $\dfrac{1}{n}$.

| Written | $\dfrac{1}{2}$ | $\dfrac{1}{3}$ | $\dfrac{1}{4}$ | $\dfrac{1}{5}$ | $\dfrac{1}{6}$ |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Read | one half | one third | one quarter | one fifth | one sixth |

To find one-$n$th of a number, divide that number by $n$.
:::

This works because splitting the number into $n$ equal parts makes each part exactly one-$n$th of it. For example, $12$ sweets split into $3$ equal parts give $12\div3=4$ in each part, so $\dfrac{1}{3}$ of $12$ is $4$.

## Examples

We start by naming one part.

:::example A piece of string
A piece of string is cut into $3$ equal lengths. What fraction of the string is each length?

**Solution.** The string is split into $3$ equal parts, so each length is one third of the string:
$$\dfrac{1}{3}.$$
So each length is $\dfrac{1}{3}$ of the string.
:::

Now practise reading and writing.

:::example Read and write
a) Write "one seventh" with numbers.

b) Read $\dfrac{1}{8}$.

**Solution.** The bottom number is the number of equal parts and the top number is the $1$ part we take.

a) One seventh is written $\dfrac{1}{7}$.

b) $\dfrac{1}{8}$ is read "one eighth".
:::

Next, find one-$n$th of a number.

:::example Find $\dfrac{1}{4}$ of $20$
**Solution.** To find one quarter of $20$, we divide $20$ by $4$:
$$20\div4=5.$$
**Check.** $4\times5=20$. Correct.

So $\dfrac{1}{4}$ of $20$ is $5$.
:::

Do the same with a bigger number.

:::example Find $\dfrac{1}{6}$ of $42$
**Solution.** We divide $42$ by $6$:
$$42\div6=7.$$
**Check.** $6\times7=42$. Correct.

So $\dfrac{1}{6}$ of $42$ is $7$.
:::

Finally, use this method for a problem from everyday life.

:::example Sharing oranges
Mum has $18$ oranges and gives her child $\dfrac{1}{3}$ of them. How many oranges does the child get?

**Solution.** The child gets one third of $18$ oranges, so we divide $18$ by $3$:
$$18\div3=6\ \text{(oranges)}.$$
**Check.** $3\times6=18$. Correct.

So the child gets $6$ oranges.
:::

:::warning Mistakes to watch out for
- **Parts that are not equal.** Wrong: a shape split into $4$ parts of different sizes, each part called $\dfrac{1}{4}$. Why it is wrong: $\dfrac{1}{4}$ is only used when the $4$ parts are equal. Right: split it into $4$ equal parts.
- **Multiplying instead of dividing.** Wrong: $\dfrac{1}{3}$ of $12$ is $12\times3=36$. Why it is wrong: one third of $12$ cannot be bigger than $12$. Right: $12\div3=4$.
- **Mixing up small and big.** Wrong: $\dfrac{1}{8}$ is bigger than $\dfrac{1}{4}$ because $8>4$. Why it is wrong: splitting into $8$ parts makes each part smaller than splitting into $4$. Right: $\dfrac{1}{4}$ is bigger than $\dfrac{1}{8}$.
- **Forgetting the unit.** Wrong: "Answer: $6$". Right: "Answer: $6$ oranges".
:::

## Summary

- Split a shape (or a number) into $n$ **equal** parts: each part is $\dfrac{1}{n}$ of it.
- $\dfrac{1}{2}$ is one half, $\dfrac{1}{3}$ is one third, $\dfrac{1}{4}$ is one quarter.
- The more equal parts, the smaller each part.
- One-$n$th of a number is that number divided by $n$.
- Check with multiplication; write the unit in the answer.

:::exercise
Each shape is split into equal parts. Write the fraction for one part:

a) A shape split into $2$ equal parts.

b) A shape split into $3$ equal parts.

c) A shape split into $8$ equal parts.

::hint
**What you need:** split into $n$ equal parts: each part is $\dfrac{1}{n}$.

**Steps:**
1. Count the equal parts.
2. Write $1$ over that number.

::solution
a) $\dfrac{1}{2}$.

b) $\dfrac{1}{3}$.

c) $\dfrac{1}{8}$.

**Answer.** a) $\dfrac{1}{2}$; b) $\dfrac{1}{3}$; c) $\dfrac{1}{8}$.
:::

a) One quarter.

**What you need:** "one-$n$th" is written $\dfrac{1}{n}$.

**Steps:**
1. Find the number $n$ in the name.
2. Write $1$ over $n$.

::solution
a) One quarter is one fourth, written $\dfrac{1}{4}$.

**Answer.** a) $\dfrac{1}{4}$; b) $\dfrac{1}{6}$; c) $\dfrac{1}{10}$.

:::exercise
Remember the last lesson. Find $\square$ when $\square\times3=15$. Then find $\dfrac{1}{3}$ of $15$.

::hint
**What you need:** the missing factor is the product divided by the known factor. One third of a number is that number divided by $3$.

**Steps:**
1. Find $\square$ by dividing.
2. Work out $15\div3$ and compare.

::solution
$\square=15\div3=5$. One third of $15$ is $15\div3=5$, the same as $\square$.

**Answer.** $\square=5$ and $\dfrac{1}{3}$ of $15$ is $5$.
:::

:::exercise
Find:

a) $\dfrac{1}{2}$ of $16$.

b) $\dfrac{1}{5}$ of $30$.

c) $\dfrac{1}{9}$ of $54$.

::hint
**What you need:** one-$n$th of a number is that number divided by $n$.

**Steps:**
1. Find $n$.
2. Divide the number by $n$, then check.

::solution
a) $16\div2=8$.

b) $30\div5=6$.

c) $54\div9=6$.

**Answer.** a) $8$; b) $6$; c) $6$.
:::

:::exercise
a) To find $\dfrac{1}{4}$ of $28$, do you multiply or divide? Why? Then work it out.

b) One cake is shared equally among $2$ friends, and an identical cake is shared equally among $4$ friends. Who gets the bigger piece? Why?

::hint
**What you need:** one-$n$th of a number is that number divided by $n$; the more equal parts, the smaller each part.

**Steps:**
1. How many equal parts do we split into?
2. Compare the number of parts in the two cases.

::solution
a) We divide, because splitting $28$ into $4$ equal parts gives parts that are each $\dfrac{1}{4}$ of $28$: $28\div4=7$.

b) A friend in the group of $2$ gets $\dfrac{1}{2}$ of a cake, a friend in the group of $4$ gets $\dfrac{1}{4}$. Fewer parts means bigger parts, so $\dfrac{1}{2}$ is bigger than $\dfrac{1}{4}$.

**Answer.** a) Divide, result $7$; b) a friend who gets $\dfrac{1}{2}$ of a cake has the bigger piece.
:::

:::exercise
Nam wrote: "$\dfrac{1}{3}$ of $12$ is $36$ because $12\times3=36$". Where did Nam go wrong? Correct it.

::hint
**What you need:** one third of a number is that number divided by $3$.

**Steps:**
1. Can one third of $12$ be bigger than $12$?
2. Use the right operation.

::solution
Nam multiplied, but we must divide: $\dfrac{1}{3}$ of $12$ is $12\div3=4$.

**Check.** $3\times4=12$. Correct.

**Answer.** Nam used the wrong operation; the right answer is $4$.
:::

:::exercise
a) There are $24$ sweets and Lan takes $\dfrac{1}{6}$ of them. How many sweets does Lan take?

b) A piece of string $40$ cm long is cut into $5$ equal lengths. How many centimetres is each length, and what fraction of the string is it?

::hint
**What you need:** one-$n$th of a number is that number divided by $n$.

**Steps:**
1. What do we know? What is asked?
2. Divide by the number of equal parts and write the answer with its unit.

::solution
a) Lan takes $24\div6=4$ (sweets).

b) Each length is $40\div5=8$ (cm) and is $\dfrac{1}{5}$ of the string.

**Answer.** a) $4$ sweets; b) $8$ cm, which is $\dfrac{1}{5}$ of the string.
:::

:::exercise Challenge
A class has $36$ pupils. $\dfrac{1}{4}$ of the pupils go to swimming club and $\dfrac{1}{9}$ of the pupils go to art club. How many more pupils go to swimming club than to art club?

::hint
**What you need:** find each part by dividing, then find the difference.

**Steps:**
1. Work out $\dfrac{1}{4}$ of $36$ and $\dfrac{1}{9}$ of $36$.
2. Subtract the two results.

::solution
The pupils at swimming club: $36\div4=9$. The pupils at art club: $36\div9=4$. The difference is $9-4=5$ (pupils).

**Answer.** $5$ pupils.
:::

## Mini test

:::quiz
::mc
A cake is shared equally among $6$ friends. What fraction of the cake does each friend get?
A. $\dfrac{1}{5}$.
B. $\dfrac{1}{6}$.
C. $6$.
D. $\dfrac{1}{7}$.
::key B
The cake is split into $6$ equal parts, so each part is $\dfrac{1}{6}$. Option C is the mistake of writing the number of parts instead of a fraction.

::mc

A. $54$.
B. $15$.
C. $6$.
D. $3$.
::key C
We divide: $18\div3=6$. Option A is the mistake of multiplying $18\times3$, and option B is subtracting $18-3$.

::mc
$\dfrac{1}{8}$ of $40$ equals
A. $5$.
B. $32$.
C. $48$.
D. $8$.
::key A
$40\div8=5$. Option B is the mistake of subtracting $40-8$, and option C is adding $40+8$.

::mc
Which of $\dfrac{1}{2}$, $\dfrac{1}{3}$, $\dfrac{1}{5}$, $\dfrac{1}{9}$ is the biggest?
A. $\dfrac{1}{2}$.
B. $\dfrac{1}{3}$.
C. $\dfrac{1}{5}$.
D. $\dfrac{1}{9}$.
::key A
The fewer equal parts, the bigger each part, so $\dfrac{1}{2}$ is the biggest. Option D is the mistake of thinking that a bigger bottom number means a bigger part.
