- Know the ways of collecting data and check whether data are reasonable.

- Build a frequency table and relative frequencies from a list of data.
- Read, draw and choose a suitable bar chart, pie chart or line graph for a given purpose.

Every day you meet numbers: test marks, temperatures, electricity bills, poll results. To use them for a decision, we must collect them properly, organise them neatly and draw them so that patterns become visible. These are the three jobs of statistics, and this lesson covers all three.

Suppose the school wants to buy equipment for a sports club and asks $40$ students in class 8A: "Which sport do you like best?". A pile of answer slips tells us nothing yet. How can we find out which sport to buy equipment for first?

## Let's explore

The first twelve slips read: Football, Badminton, Football, Basketball, Football, Swimming, Badminton, Football, Shuttlecock kicking, Badminton, Football, Basketball. Count how many times each sport appears.

Making a tally mark for each answer, we get: Football $5$ times, Badminton $3$ times, Basketball $2$ times, Swimming $1$ time, Shuttlecock kicking $1$ time. The total is $5+3+2+1+1=12$, exactly the number of slips. A long list has become a short table, and we see at once that football is chosen most often. The next sections name and organise each of these steps.

## Collecting data

**Data** are pieces of information (numbers, names, ...) that we record to study a question. There are two main ways to collect them.

- **Directly:** observing, measuring, interviewing or handing out a questionnaire yourself, for example measuring the heights of the students in your class.
- **Indirectly:** taking data that already exist in books, newspapers or the website of a statistics office.

Data are useful only if they are **reasonable**: from the right group of people, in the right units and within a range that is possible. A test mark out of $10$ cannot be $12$.

:::example Choosing a method of collection
Suggest a suitable way to collect data for each question:

a) The number of books each student in your class read this month.

b) The population of a province last year.

**Solution.** a) We hand out a questionnaire or ask each student (direct collection), because these data do not exist anywhere yet.

b) We look them up in an official source such as the yearbook of a statistics office (indirect collection), because we cannot count the people of a whole province ourselves.

So a) direct; b) indirect.
:::

:::example Checking whether data are reasonable
The test marks (out of $10$) of six students are recorded as $7$; $8.5$; $9$; $12$; $6$; $7.5$. Which value is not reasonable? Why?

**Solution.** The highest possible mark on a scale of $10$ is $10$. We see that $12>10$, so the value $12$ is not reasonable; it may be a recording error (for example $1.2$ or $2$). All the other values lie between $0$ and $10$.

So the value $12$ is not reasonable and must be checked.
:::

## Classifying data

:::definition Qualitative and quantitative data
- **Qualitative data** are not numbers; they describe a quality (a name, a colour, a favourite subject, ...).
- **Quantitative data** are numbers that measure or count something (height, test marks, number of siblings, ...).
:::

**Going deeper.** A number is not always quantitative data. A telephone number or a footballer's shirt number is only a *label*; adding them or finding their mean has no meaning. Quantitative data must be numbers for which calculations make sense.

:::example Classifying data
State whether each kind of data is qualitative or quantitative:

a) Favourite dish.

b) Mass (kg).

c) Telephone number.

d) Number of brothers and sisters.

**Solution.** We ask whether the data are a quantity that can be measured or counted.

a) A dish is a name, not a number: qualitative.

b) Mass is a measured quantity: quantitative.

c) A telephone number is only a label, and calculations on it have no meaning: qualitative.

d) The number of siblings is a counted quantity: quantitative.

So a) and c) are qualitative; b) and d) are quantitative.
:::

## Representing data in tables

To see the data quickly, we build a **frequency table**. The **frequency** of a value is the number of times it occurs. The **relative frequency** is the frequency divided by the total number of data values, usually written as a percentage:
$$\text{relative frequency}=\frac{\text{frequency}}{\text{total number of data values}}.$$
The frequencies add up to the total number of data values, and the relative frequencies add up to $100\%$.

:::example Building a frequency table
Twenty students in class 8A were asked how they travel to school and answered: Walk, Bike, Bus, Motorbike, Motorbike, Walk, Bike, Bike, Motorbike, Bus, Walk, Motorbike, Bike, Motorbike, Bus, Bike, Motorbike, Walk, Motorbike, Bike. (Motorbike means a lift on a family member's motorbike.) Build a table of frequencies and relative frequencies.

**Solution.** We count how many times each method appears, then divide by the total number of students, $20$:

| Way to school | Walk | Bike | Bus | Motorbike | Total |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Frequency | $4$ | $6$ | $3$ | $7$ | $20$ |
| Relative frequency | $20\%$ | $30\%$ | $15\%$ | $35\%$ | $100\%$ |

Check: $4+6+3+7=20$ and $20+30+15+35=100$.

So the motorbike is the most common way to school ($35\%$).
:::

## Representing data in charts

A chart lets us compare at a glance. Each kind of chart has its own strength:

| Chart | Use it to |
|---|---|
| Bar chart | compare quantities between groups |
| Double bar chart | compare two data sets on the same groups (for example boys and girls in each class) |
| Pictogram | compare quantities with symbols; easy to read for small numbers |
| Pie chart | show the proportion of each part of a whole |
| Line graph | follow a change over time |

We draw a bar chart for the table in the example above: each bar stands for one way to school, and its height equals the frequency.

:::example Drawing a bar chart
Draw a bar chart for the frequency table of ways to school above.

**Solution.** We draw two axes; on the horizontal axis we place the ways to school, and on the vertical axis we mark the frequency. The bars have the same width, equal gaps, and heights $4$; $6$; $3$; $7$.

So we get the chart below, where the "Motorbike" bar is the tallest and the "Bus" bar is the shortest.
:::

A pie chart divides a circle into sectors; the angle of the sector for a group equals its relative frequency times $360^\circ$.

:::example Angles of a pie chart
Find the angles of the pie chart for the frequency table of ways to school above.

**Solution.** The whole circle stands for $100\%$, that is $360^\circ$, so each $1\%$ stands for $3.6^\circ$. We have:

$$\begin{aligned} \text{Walk: } & 20\%\cdot360^\circ=72^\circ, \\ \text{Bike: } & 30\%\cdot360^\circ=108^\circ, \\ \text{Bus: } & 15\%\cdot360^\circ=54^\circ, \\ \text{Motorbike: } & 35\%\cdot360^\circ=126^\circ. \end{aligned}$$

Check: $72+108+54+126=360$.

So the sector angles are $72^\circ$, $108^\circ$, $54^\circ$, $126^\circ$.
:::

Now we look at data that change over time, where a line graph is the right tool.

:::example Electricity use by month
Suppose a family records its electricity use (in kWh) for the first six months of the year: $180$; $210$; $260$; $290$; $270$; $230$. Draw a line graph, and say which month had the highest use and by how many kWh the use rose from month $1$ to that month.

**Solution.** We put the month on the horizontal axis and the electricity use on the vertical axis, plot the six points and join consecutive points with line segments.

The graph is shown below. The highest point is at month $4$ ($290$ kWh), and from month $1$ to month $4$ the use rose by $290-180=110$ (kWh).

So month $4$ had the highest use, and it was $110$ kWh more than in month $1$.
:::

:::warning Mistakes to watch out for
- **Treating every number as quantitative data.** Wrong: "a telephone number is quantitative data". Why it is wrong: it is only a label, and a total or mean of telephone numbers is meaningless. Right: it is qualitative data.
- **Relative frequencies that do not add up to $100\%$.** Wrong: $20\%+30\%+15\%+30\%=95\%$ (one value copied wrongly). Why it is wrong: if the groups cover everything without overlap, the total must be $100\%$. Right: recount, $20\%+30\%+15\%+35\%=100\%$.
- **Using a pie chart for data over time.** Wrong: showing the temperature every hour with a pie chart. Why it is wrong: a pie chart shows only the proportions of a whole, not a trend. Right: use a line graph.
:::

## Summary

- Data are collected directly (observing, questionnaires) or indirectly (books, newspapers, official sources), and must be reasonable.
- Qualitative data are not numbers; quantitative data are numbers for which calculations make sense.
- Relative frequency is frequency divided by the total; the relative frequencies add up to $100\%$.
- A bar chart compares groups, a pie chart shows proportions, a line graph shows a trend over time.
- The angle of a pie chart sector is the relative frequency times $360^\circ$.

## Practice

Each exercise comes with what you need and how to start. Try it yourself first, then open the solution.

:::exercise
State whether each kind of data is qualitative or quantitative:

a) Eye colour of the students in a class.

b) Height (cm) of the students.

c) Number of students in each class of a school.

d) Names of favourite songs.

::hint
**What you need:** quantitative data are measured or counted numbers.

**Steps:**
1. Ask: are these numbers for which calculations make sense?
2. Classify each kind of data.

::solution
a) Eye colour is a name: qualitative. b) Height is a measurement: quantitative. c) The number of students is a count: quantitative. d) Song names: qualitative.

So a) and d) are qualitative; b) and c) are quantitative.

**Answer.** a) qualitative; b) quantitative; c) quantitative; d) qualitative.
:::

:::exercise
Suggest a suitable way to collect data (directly or indirectly) for each question:

a) The time each student in your class takes to travel from home to school.

b) The average yearly rainfall of a city over the last $10$ years.

::hint
**What you need:** collect directly when you can measure or ask yourself; indirectly when another organisation has already recorded the data.

**Steps:**
1. Ask: can we collect these data ourselves?
2. If not, where could they come from?

::solution
a) We ask each student or hand out a questionnaire: direct collection.

b) We cannot go back in time to measure, so we look up the weather service's records: indirect collection.

So a) direct; b) indirect.

**Answer.** a) direct; b) indirect.
:::

:::exercise
The numbers of boys in five classes in grade 8 are recorded as $18$; $20$; $2.5$; $19$; $22$. Which value is not reasonable? Why?

::hint
**What you need:** a number of people must be a whole number.

**Steps:**
1. Ask: can there be half a student?
2. Point out the unsuitable value.

::solution
The number of boys is a count, so it must be a whole number. We see that $2.5$ is not a whole number, so it is not reasonable; it may be a recording error (perhaps $25$).

So the value $2.5$ is not reasonable.

**Answer.** $2.5$.
:::

:::exercise
The numbers of brothers and sisters of $20$ students are recorded as $1$, $2$, $0$, $1$, $1$, $3$, $2$, $1$, $0$, $2$, $1$, $1$, $2$, $0$, $1$, $2$, $1$, $0$, $1$, $2$. Build a table of frequencies and relative frequencies.

::hint
**What you need:** frequency is the number of occurrences; relative frequency is the frequency divided by $20$.

**Steps:**
1. Count how many times $0$, $1$, $2$, $3$ occur.
2. Divide by $20$ and write as a percentage.

::solution
We count and divide by $20$:

| Number of siblings | $0$ | $1$ | $2$ | $3$ | Total |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Frequency | $4$ | $9$ | $6$ | $1$ | $20$ |
| Relative frequency | $20\%$ | $45\%$ | $30\%$ | $5\%$ | $100\%$ |

Check: $4+9+6+1=20$.

**Answer.** Frequencies $4$; $9$; $6$; $1$, relative frequencies $20\%$; $45\%$; $30\%$; $5\%$.
:::

:::exercise
From the frequency table in the previous exercise, describe the bar chart for the number of siblings (the heights of the bars and which bar is tallest).

::hint
**What you need:** the height of a bar equals the frequency.

**Steps:**
1. Each value $0$, $1$, $2$, $3$ has its own bar.
2. Compare the frequencies.

::solution
The bars for $0$, $1$, $2$, $3$ siblings have heights $4$; $9$; $6$; $1$. The bar for $1$ sibling is the tallest ($9$) and the bar for $3$ siblings is the shortest ($1$).

So most students have $1$ brother or sister.

**Answer.** Bar heights: $4$; $9$; $6$; $1$.
:::

:::exercise
A survey of $40$ students about their favourite type of book gives: Stories $16$, Science $12$, Poetry $4$, Other $8$. Find the relative frequencies and the matching sector angles of a pie chart.

::hint
**What you need:** the sector angle is the relative frequency times $360^\circ$.

**Steps:**
1. Divide each frequency by $40$.
2. Multiply by $360^\circ$.

::solution
We have: Stories $\dfrac{16}{40}=40\%$, angle $144^\circ$; Science $\dfrac{12}{40}=30\%$, angle $108^\circ$; Poetry $\dfrac{4}{40}=10\%$, angle $36^\circ$; Other $\dfrac{8}{40}=20\%$, angle $72^\circ$.

Check: $144+108+36+72=360$.

**Answer.** $40\%$, $30\%$, $10\%$, $20\%$; angles $144^\circ$, $108^\circ$, $36^\circ$, $72^\circ$.
:::

:::exercise
Choose the most suitable chart (bar chart, double bar chart, pie chart, line graph) for each purpose:

a) Compare the number of students in four classes of grade 8.

b) Follow the outdoor temperature every hour during a day.

c) Show the percentage of each item in a family's budget.

d) Compare the number of boys and girls in each of four classes.

::hint
**What you need:** see the table of charts above.

**Steps:**
1. Decide the purpose: comparison, proportion or trend?
2. Choose the matching chart.

::solution
a) Comparing groups: bar chart. b) Over time: line graph. c) Proportions of a whole: pie chart. d) Two data sets on the same groups: double bar chart.

**Answer.** a) bar chart; b) line graph; c) pie chart; d) double bar chart.
:::

:::exercise
Suppose a family spends $15$ million dong a month: food $40\%$, housing $25\%$, education $15\%$, other $20\%$. Find the amount for each item and the angle of the "housing" sector in a pie chart.

::hint
**What you need:** amount equals percentage times total; angle equals percentage times $360^\circ$.

**Steps:**
1. Work out $15\cdot\dfrac{p}{100}$ for each item.
2. Work out $25\%\cdot360^\circ$.

::solution
We have: food $15\cdot0.4=6$ (million dong); housing $15\cdot0.25=3.75$ (million dong); education $15\cdot0.15=2.25$ (million dong); other $15\cdot0.2=3$ (million dong). The housing angle is $25\%\cdot360^\circ=90^\circ$.

Check: $6+3.75+2.25+3=15$.

So the items are $6$; $3.75$; $2.25$; $3$ million dong and the housing sector is $90^\circ$.

**Answer.** $6$; $3.75$; $2.25$; $3$ million dong; $90^\circ$.
:::

:::exercise
Suppose a student records the number of glasses of water drunk each day for five days: $6$; $8$; $5$; $8$; $7$. Build a frequency table of the values and say which value occurs most often.

::hint
**What you need:** frequency is the number of times a value occurs.

**Steps:**
1. List the different values.
2. Count how many times each occurs.

::solution
The different values are $5$, $6$, $7$, $8$ with frequencies $1$, $1$, $1$, $2$; the total is $5$.

So the value $8$ occurs most often ($2$ times).

**Answer.** Frequencies $1$; $1$; $1$; $2$; the value $8$.
:::

:::exercise Challenge
A survey of $50$ students about their favourite subject is shown in a pie chart. The sector for Maths has angle $108^\circ$; English takes $24\%$; Science has $10$ students; the rest chose other subjects.

a) How many students chose Maths?

b) How many students chose other subjects, and what percentage is that?

::hint
**What you need:** the number of students is $\dfrac{\text{angle}}{360^\circ}\cdot50$ or percentage times $50$.

**Steps:**
1. a) Find the relative frequency of Maths from the angle $108^\circ$.
2. b) Find the number for English, then subtract the known groups from $50$.

::solution
a) The relative frequency of Maths is $\dfrac{108}{360}=30\%$, so $50\cdot0.3=15$ students chose Maths.

b) English has $50\cdot0.24=12$ students. The number choosing other subjects is $50-15-12-10=13$, which is $\dfrac{13}{50}=26\%$.

So $15$ students chose Maths and $13$ students ($26\%$) chose other subjects.

**Answer.** a) $15$ students. b) $13$ students, $26\%$.
:::
