Almost every student feels nervous before an important maths exam. That is normal, and a little tension is useful: it sharpens attention and gives you energy. Trouble starts only when worry takes up the space your working memory needs for mathematics. So how can you feel nervous and still do well? This lesson collects practical habits that help you stay in control. It is not medical advice. If worry or low mood lasts for weeks, or affects sleep, eating or daily life, talk to a parent, a teacher, a school counsellor or a doctor.

## Why nerves can lower your score

Solving a multi-step problem uses **working memory**: the small mental space where you hold the numbers and the plan. Anxious thoughts such as "I am going to fail" compete for that same space. That is why a student can know a method perfectly at home and forget it in the exam room. It does not mean you are weak at maths. The good news is that the methods below aim at exactly this problem: they move the worry out of working memory, or change how you read the body's alarm signals.

## Two techniques with research behind them

**1. Write your worries down before the exam.** Ramirez and Beilock (2011) published studies in *Science*, including one with ninth-grade students before their final exam. They asked students to spend about ten minutes before an important test writing freely about their thoughts and feelings about it. Students who usually felt very anxious about tests did better after writing than similar students who did not write. A likely explanation is that once the worries are on paper, working memory is freed.

How to use it: the evening before, or in the waiting area, take a sheet of paper and write for ten minutes about what you are worried about, as honestly as you can. Then put it away.

**2. Call it energy, not fear.** Jamieson and colleagues (2010) studied students preparing for the GRE, a graduate admission test. The students were told that the physical arousal you feel during a test, such as a faster heartbeat, does not hurt performance and can even help it. They scored higher on the maths section than students who were not given this message, both on a practice test and later on the real exam.

How to use it: when you notice the signs of nerves, tell yourself "this is my body getting ready", and turn your attention to the first question.

## The last week

- **Keep the plan light.** Mixed review and the [error log](lesson:error-log), and no new topics.
- **Rehearse the real conditions** once or twice: the same time of day, the same calculator, a full paper.
- **Keep your normal sleep schedule.** Sleep supports memory, and the last week is not the time to stay up late cramming.
- **Prepare the logistics**: the route, the documents, the allowed calculator with fresh batteries, the pens.

## The night before and the morning

| When | Do | Avoid |
|---|---|---|
| Evening before | A short look at your formula list; pack your bag; something relaxing | A full new paper; comparing with friends who "studied everything" |
| Bedtime | Your usual time | Staying up to study one more topic |
| Morning | A normal breakfast; leave with time to spare | Learning a new method in the waiting area |
| Waiting | The ten-minute worry writing; slow breathing | Listening to rumors about the paper |

## In the exam room

If your mind goes blank, do not panic: it happens to many students, and there is a way through it. Try these steps:

1. **Stop for 30 seconds.** Put the pen down and breathe slowly: breathe in through your nose, then breathe out for longer than you breathed in, a few times.
2. **Find an easy point to restart.** Go to a question you are sure about and do it. Once one question is done, memory starts to come back.
3. **Write what you know.** On the hard question, write the given facts and the first formula that relates them. Putting pen to paper starts the thinking.
4. **Use the two-pass method** from [Strategy in the exam room](lesson:exam-day-strategy). There is no need to win every question in order.

:::tip Talk to yourself like a coach
Replace "I can't do this" with a small plan: "I will do question 5 first, then come back." Short, practical sentences work better than general encouragement.
:::

## For parents

Your calm is contagious. Before an exam, the most helpful messages are about effort and preparation ("you have practised this"), not about results or comparisons. After the exam, ask how your child is, not what the score will be. If your child's worry seems much stronger than usual, or does not fade after the exam season, ask for help from the school or a health professional. Nerves before an exam are normal, and there are concrete ways to get through them.
