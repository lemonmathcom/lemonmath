description: A practical 12-week maths revision plan for any exam: diagnose, learn topic by topic, mix topics, then sit timed papers, with a weekly template.

---

:::goals
- Divide the 12 weeks before an exam into three phases with clear aims.
- Turn a syllabus into a topic list ranked by weight and by weakness.
- Plan a realistic week that includes new topics, old topics and practice papers.
- Adjust the plan when you fall behind instead of giving up on it.
:::

Look honestly at how you revise. Have you ever spent three weeks on the topic you already liked, nothing on the one you feared, and saved every past paper for the last weekend? Many students do not fail an exam because they did not work. They fail because the work was spread badly. A plan fixes that. Twelve weeks is a good length for a major exam such as the grade 10 entrance exam, the national graduation exam, the SAT or an AP exam. With less time, shorten each phase in proportion.

## Three phases

| Weeks | Phase | Main aim | Share of time on full papers |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Diagnose | Find out where you stand | one full paper |
| 2–7 | Learn and repair | Cover every topic once, weakest and heaviest first | about 10% |
| 8–10 | Mix | Practise topics together, as the exam mixes them | about 30% |
| 11–12 | Rehearse | Timed papers under real conditions | about 60% |

The idea behind this shape comes from the research in [Revision that works](lesson:evidence-based-revision). You learn each topic once, then return to it several times with gaps in between, and the closer the exam gets, the more of your time goes on **retrieving** knowledge in exam conditions.

## Week 1: diagnose

1. Find the official description of your exam: the topics, the number of questions and the time. On Lemonmath, every exam has an overview lesson with this information.
2. Sit one complete paper under real timing, before you revise anything. If an official paper is available, use that.
3. Mark it and put every lost mark into a table like this one:

| Topic | Weight in the exam | Marks lost | Priority |
|---|---|---|---|
| Quadratic functions | high | 6 | 1 |
| Probability | medium | 4 | 2 |
| Geometry | medium | 1 | 4 |
| Statistics | low | 3 | 3 |

This is how to set priorities: a topic with high weight and many lost marks goes first. A topic with low weight and few lost marks can wait.

:::tip Be honest, not harsh
The first paper is only meant to show you where the gaps are. A low score in week 1 is information, not a verdict, so do not be hard on yourself. Students who skip the diagnosis usually spend their weeks on what they already know, because it feels good. Many students fall into this.
:::

## Weeks 2 to 7: learn and repair

Now we take the topics in priority order. A good rhythm is two topics per week. For each topic, we work like this:

- **Day 1:** study the lesson, follow the worked examples, then do the similar exercises.
- **Day 2 or 3:** write what you remember on a blank page, then do the application exercises and the mini test.
- **One week later:** do five mixed questions on the topic inside a later session.
Why a blank page? Because only when you recall something yourself do you find out what you really remember.

Keep every mistake in an [error log](lesson:error-log) from the first week. Do not wait to "finish the syllabus" before going back to old topics, because by then the old material will have faded. From week 3, the first 15 minutes of each session are for a topic from an earlier week.

## Weeks 8 to 10: mix

At this stage the exam format matters more than the topic list, because the real exam mixes the topics together. Build sessions out of mixed sets: 15 to 25 questions from different topics, in random order, with a time limit about 10% shorter than the real pace. Once a week, sit a full paper. After each paper, spend at least as long reviewing as you spent writing it.

## Weeks 11 and 12: rehearse

- Sit two or three full papers per week at the same time of day as the real exam.
- Use only the tools you will have on the day: the same calculator, the same reference sheet, no notes.
- Use the time between papers to redo the problems in your error log, and do not start new topics.
- In the last two days, stop full papers. Review your formula list and your error log, and sleep as usual.

## A sample week in the "learn and repair" phase

| Day | 45–60 minutes |
|---|---|
| Monday | 15 min old topic · new topic A: lesson and examples |
| Tuesday | 15 min old topic · topic A: exercises |
| Wednesday | Blank page on A · new topic B: lesson and examples |
| Thursday | Mini test A · topic B: exercises |
| Friday | Mini test B · mixed set of 15 questions |
| Saturday | Error log: redo every problem from this week |
| Sunday | Rest, or a short catch-up if the week was disrupted |

## When the plan breaks

Every plan breaks at some point: illness, a school trip, a bad week. This is very common, so do not worry. When it happens, we do this:

1. **Do not move everything back.** The exam date does not move. Instead, drop the lowest-priority topic from your table.
2. **Protect the mixed and rehearsal phases.** If you must cut, shorten weeks 2 to 7, not weeks 8 to 12.
3. **Keep the error log going.** Even 20 minutes a day on old mistakes keeps your progress alive.

:::note For parents
A child's plan works best when it is written down and visible, with sessions short enough to finish. A parent can ask "What did you get wrong this week, and what did you do about it?" rather than "How many hours did you study?", because that is the more useful question.
:::
