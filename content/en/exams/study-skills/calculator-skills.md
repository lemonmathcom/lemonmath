:::goals
- Know which kind of calculator each major maths exam allows or requires.
- Use the functions of a scientific calculator that save the most time: tables, equation solvers, integrals and statistics.
- Use Desmos or a graphing calculator to graph, find zeros and intersections, and check answers.
- Recognize the situations where a calculator wastes time or hides a mistake.
:::

A calculator is part of almost every maths exam today, and students who use it well gain both time and safety. But a calculator only answers the question you type. The skill is knowing **what** to type, **when** it is faster than thinking, and **how** to use it to check work done by hand.

## What each exam allows

| Exam | Calculator |
|---|---|
| Vietnam grade 10 entrance and graduation exams | A scientific calculator from the models allowed by the regulations (no text memory, no internet). Many students use a Casio fx-580VN X. |
| SAT | Allowed on every question. Desmos is built into Bluebook; you may also bring an approved calculator. |
| ACT | Allowed on the whole Math section; some models are prohibited. |
| AP Calculus, AP Precalculus, AP Statistics | A graphing calculator is required for the calculator parts; other parts forbid any calculator. |
| IB Maths AA | Paper 1 forbids calculators; Papers 2 and 3 require a graphic display calculator (GDC). IB Maths AI uses the GDC on every paper. |
| Cambridge IGCSE Mathematics 0580 | One paper without a calculator and one with a scientific calculator, at each tier. |

Rules about permitted models change and are published by each exam board. Before you register, check the official list, and practise on the exact model you will use on the day.

## A scientific calculator: four functions worth mastering

These are available on common scientific calculators such as the Casio fx-580VN X; the key names may differ on other models.

1. **Table of values** (TABLE mode). Enter $f(x)$, a start, an end and a step. Use it to estimate maximum and minimum values, to see where a function changes sign, and to check a whole row of an answer at once.
2. **Equation and system solvers.** Polynomial equations of low degree and systems of linear equations are solved directly. Use them to check, not to replace, your algebra: the exam marks the method in written questions.
3. **Integrals and derivatives at a point.** A definite integral or the derivative at a given $x$ is computed numerically. In multiple-choice questions on integrals, this is the fastest way to test the options.
4. **Statistics.** Enter a data list, or a frequency table, and read the mean, the standard deviation and, with two variables, the regression line.

:::example Testing options with the integral key
Which number equals $\displaystyle\int_0^2(3x^2-2x+1)\,dx$?
A. $2$
B. $6$
C. $8$
D. $10$

**Solution.** By hand: $\Big[x^3-x^2+x\Big]_0^2=8-4+2=6$. On the calculator, the integral key gives $6$ in a few seconds. The answer is **B**. Use the calculator after solving by hand, or when the antiderivative is hard to find.
:::

## Desmos and graphing calculators

For the SAT, AP and IB, a graph is often faster than algebra. Five tasks to practise until they are automatic:

- **Graph a function** in a sensible window, and read its key points (vertex, intercepts).
- **Find the zeros** of a function.
- **Find the intersection** of two graphs, which solves $f(x)=g(x)$ and systems.
- **Use a slider** for a constant $k$ and watch how the graph changes.
- **Compute statistics** and a line of best fit from a table of data.

For AP Calculus the course description expects four calculator skills: plotting a graph in a chosen window, finding zeros, computing a numerical derivative at a point and computing a definite integral numerically. In calculator-active free-response questions, write the setup (for example the integral) before giving the number, and give decimals correct to three places.

:::tip One habit for every graphing tool
When the graph shows something surprising, change the window before you trust it. A parabola can look like a straight line, and two curves can look as if they touch when they do not.
:::

## When not to use the calculator

- **When mental arithmetic is faster.** $3x+7=22$ needs no machine.
- **When the answer must be exact.** If the options are $2\sqrt3$ or $\dfrac{\pi}{4}$, a decimal like $3.464$ still has to be matched; it is often faster to keep the algebra exact.
- **When the question tests reasoning.** "Which statement must be true?" or "explain why" cannot be answered by a number.
- **When you are tired of thinking.** Typing long expressions under stress is where most calculator errors happen: a missing bracket changes everything.

:::warning Common calculator mistakes
- **Degrees or radians.** Check the angle mode before any trigonometry.
- **Brackets.** $-3^2=-9$ but $(-3)^2=9$; $\dfrac{1}{2x}$ must be typed as `1/(2x)`.
- **Rounding too early.** Keep full precision in intermediate steps and round only the final answer.
- **Decimal separator.** The SAT and AP use a point ($2.5$); Vietnamese exams use a comma ($2{,}5$). Make sure you write the final answer in the form the exam expects.
:::

## Practise with the same tool

The best preparation is simple: do every practice paper with the calculator you will use on the day, and add calculator errors to your [error log](lesson:error-log) like any other mistake. After a few weeks, the keys you need will be where your fingers expect them, and you will spend exam time on mathematics instead of menus.
