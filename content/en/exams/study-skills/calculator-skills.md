:::goals
- Know which kind of calculator each major maths exam allows or requires.
- Use the functions of a scientific calculator that save the most time: tables, equation solvers, integrals and statistics.
- Use Desmos or a graphing calculator to graph, find zeros and intersections, and check answers.
- Recognize the situations where a calculator wastes time or hides a mistake.
:::

A calculator is part of almost every maths exam today, and used well it saves you both time and mistakes. But it only answers the question you type. So the skill is knowing **what** to type, **when** it is faster than thinking, and **how** to use it to check work you have done by hand.

## What each exam allows

| Exam | Calculator |
|---|---|
| Vietnam grade 10 entrance and graduation exams | A scientific calculator from the models allowed by the regulations (no text memory, no internet). Many students use a Casio fx-580VN X. |
| SAT | Allowed on every question. Desmos is built into Bluebook; you may also bring an approved calculator. |
| ACT | Allowed on the whole Math section; some models are prohibited. |
| AP Calculus, AP Precalculus, AP Statistics | A graphing calculator is required for the calculator parts; other parts forbid any calculator. |
| IB Maths AA | Paper 1 forbids calculators; Papers 2 and 3 require a graphic display calculator (GDC). IB Maths AI uses the GDC on every paper. |
| Cambridge IGCSE Mathematics 0580 | One paper without a calculator and one with a scientific calculator, at each tier. |

Rules about which models are allowed change, and each exam board publishes its own. Before you register, check the official list. Then practise on the exact model you will use on the day, so your fingers already know it.

## A scientific calculator: four functions worth mastering

These four are available on common scientific calculators such as the Casio fx-580VN X. The key names may differ on other models, so look for the matching function.

1. **Table of values** (TABLE mode). Enter $f(x)$, a start, an end and a step, and the calculator lists the values for you. Use it to estimate maximum and minimum values, to see where a function changes sign, and to check a whole row of an answer at once.
2. **Equation and system solvers.** Polynomial equations of low degree and systems of linear equations are solved directly. Use them to check your algebra, not to replace it: the exam marks the method in written questions.
3. **Integrals and derivatives at a point.** The calculator finds a definite integral, or the derivative at a given $x$, numerically. In multiple-choice questions on integrals, this is the fastest way to test the options.
4. **Statistics.** Enter a data list or a frequency table, then read the mean and the standard deviation. With two variables, you can also read the regression line.

:::example Testing options with the integral key
Which number equals $\displaystyle\int_0^2(3x^2-2x+1)\,dx$?
A. $2$
B. $6$
C. $8$
D. $10$

**Solution.** By hand: $\Big[x^3-x^2+x\Big]_0^2=8-4+2=6$. On the calculator, the integral key gives $6$ in a few seconds. The answer is **B**. Use the calculator after solving by hand, or when the antiderivative is hard to find.
:::

## Desmos and graphing calculators

For the SAT, AP and IB, looking at a graph is often faster than doing the algebra. Here are five tasks to practise until they are automatic:

- **Graph a function** in a sensible window, and read its key points (vertex, intercepts).
- **Find the zeros** of a function.
- **Find the intersection** of two graphs. This is the same as solving $f(x)=g(x)$, or a system of equations.
- **Use a slider** for a constant $k$ and watch how the graph changes.
- **Compute statistics** and a line of best fit from a table of data.

For AP Calculus the course description expects four calculator skills: plotting a graph in a chosen window, finding zeros, computing a numerical derivative at a point and computing a definite integral numerically. In calculator-active free-response questions, write the setup first (for example the integral), then give the number. Give decimals correct to three places.

:::tip One habit for every graphing tool
When a graph shows something surprising, change the window before you trust it. A parabola can look like a straight line, and two curves can look as if they touch when they do not.
:::

## When not to use the calculator

- **When mental arithmetic is faster.** $3x+7=22$ needs no machine.
- **When the answer must be exact.** If the options are $2\sqrt3$ or $\dfrac{\pi}{4}$, a decimal like $3.464$ still has to be matched, and it is often faster to keep the algebra exact.
- **When the question tests reasoning.** A question such as "Which statement must be true?" or "explain why" cannot be answered by a number.
- **When you are tired of thinking.** Typing long expressions under stress is where most calculator errors happen: one missing bracket changes everything.

:::warning Common calculator mistakes
- **Degrees or radians.** Check the angle mode before any trigonometry.
- **Brackets.** Many students slip here: $-3^2=-9$ but $(-3)^2=9$. And $\dfrac{1}{2x}$ must be typed as `1/(2x)`.
- **Rounding too early.** Keep full precision in intermediate steps and round only the final answer.
- **Decimal separator.** The SAT and AP use a point ($2.5$); Vietnamese exams use a comma ($2{,}5$). Write the final answer in the form the exam expects.
:::

## Practise with the same tool

The best preparation is simple: do every practice paper with the calculator you will use on the day, and add calculator errors to your [error log](lesson:error-log) like any other mistake. After a few weeks, the keys you need will be where your fingers expect them, so exam time goes on mathematics instead of menus. To sum up, a calculator helps when you know what to type, when to press and how to check the result. Each practice paper is a chance to learn all three.
