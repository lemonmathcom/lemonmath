:::goals
- Plan the first five minutes and the last ten minutes of any maths exam.
- Use the two-pass method and a clear rule for when to skip a question.
- Apply different tactics to multiple-choice, true/false, short-answer and written questions.
- Check answers quickly, in ways that actually catch mistakes.
:::

Two students who know the same mathematics can finish an exam with very different marks. The difference is rarely luck; it is how they spend their time. Strategy cannot replace knowledge, but it makes sure that everything you know turns into marks. The ideas below work for the Vietnamese entrance and graduation exams as well as for the SAT, ACT, AP and IB.

## Before you start writing

- **Know the format in advance.** By exam day you should know the number of questions, the time, the parts and how each part is marked. You should not be reading the instructions for the first time.
- **Look at the whole paper first, if the format allows it.** Spend one or two minutes reading through: which questions are familiar, which look long. On a computer-based test such as the SAT, look at the question navigator instead.
- **Write down what you might forget.** A formula you always mix up, written in the margin in the first minute, saves stress later.

## The two-pass method

Most marks are lost not on the hardest question but on easy questions that were never reached, or rushed at the end. The two-pass method prevents this:

1. **First pass.** Go through the paper in order. Answer every question you can do in a reasonable time. If a question has no clear plan after about **two minutes**, mark it, choose your best guess if it is multiple choice, and move on.
2. **Second pass.** Return to the marked questions, easiest first, with the time you have left.

:::tip The two-minute rule
Two minutes is a guide, not a law: for the long final questions of a written exam, more time is normal. The rule is really about being stuck. If you have read a question twice and still do not know where to start, every extra minute there is a minute stolen from questions you can do.
:::

## Time budget

Work out a budget before the exam, so that you can check your pace at fixed points.

| Exam | Time | Pace check |
|---|---|---|
| Vietnam graduation exam (12 multiple choice, 4 true/false, 6 short answer) | 90 minutes | about 25 minutes for Part I, 30 for Part II, 30 for Part III, 5 to check |
| SAT Math module (22 questions) | 35 minutes | question 10 by about minute 12; the last 6 questions get the extra time |
| A written exam with 5 problems | 120 minutes | about 20 minutes per problem, 20 minutes to check and finish |

These are suggestions; adjust them to your own strengths after your practice papers.

## Tactics by question type

**Multiple choice.**
- Cover the options and solve first, then match. Options are designed to catch typical mistakes.
- If you are stuck, eliminate what is impossible: wrong sign, wrong size, wrong units.
- Plug the options into the question, or plug a simple number into expressions.
- Never leave a question blank when there is no penalty for wrong answers.

**True/false with four statements (Vietnam graduation exam, Part II).** Marks grow quickly with the number of correct statements: 1, 2, 3 or 4 correct statements give 0.1, 0.25, 0.5 or 1 point. Statement a) is usually the easiest, and later statements often use earlier results, so work through them in order and decide each one on its own.

**Short answer.** The answer must be one number. Read the instructions on rounding and on how to write decimals and negative numbers, and check that your number is reasonable before writing it in the boxes.

**Written solutions (entrance exams, AP free response, IB).** Marks are given for the method, not only for the answer. So:
- write each step on its own line, with the formula or theorem you use;
- if you cannot finish, write what you would do next: partial marks are common;
- state the final answer clearly, with units, and answer the question exactly as asked.

## Checking that works

Rereading your solution rarely finds a mistake, because you read what you meant to write. Better checks:

- **Substitute back.** Put the solution of an equation into the original equation.
- **Estimate.** Does the size make sense? A probability above $1$ or a negative length is a sign error, not a discovery.
- **Solve a different way.** Use a graph, a table, or a calculator check for a calculation done by hand.
- **Reread only the question.** Is the answer the quantity asked for, in the requested form?

Save the last five to ten minutes for checking, and spend them on the questions where you were least sure, not on the ones you found easy.

:::warning Common traps
- **Changing a correct answer without a reason.** Change an answer when a check shows a mistake, not because of a vague feeling.
- **Leaving the hardest question for "later" but spending the first 20 minutes on it anyway.**
- **Copying mistakes.** Check that the answer you mark on the answer sheet, or type on the screen, is the answer in your working.
:::

## After the exam

Do not repeat every question with friends straight after the exam: it changes nothing and can shake your confidence before the next paper. Rest, eat, and get ready for the next one. The detailed review belongs to practice papers, where it can still help: that is what the [error log](lesson:error-log) is for.
