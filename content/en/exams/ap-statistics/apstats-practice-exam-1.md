Taking a full exam under exam conditions is the best rehearsal there is. You find out where the time goes, which kinds of questions still slow you down, and how it feels to write clear conclusions when you are tired. This practice exam follows the AP Statistics format from May 2027. All questions are written by Lemonmath; they are not real AP questions, but they cover the five units in about the official proportions.

## How to take this exam

| | Section I | Section II |
|---|---|---|
| Questions | 42 multiple choice, 4 options each | 4 free response, 10 points each |
| Time | 90 minutes | 90 minutes |
| Tools | graphing calculator, formula sheet | graphing calculator, formula sheet |

1. Do each section with a timer and without looking at the solutions. Submit Section I to see your score.
2. Questions 14–16 and 36–38 are sets that share one stimulus, as on the real exam.
3. Write the free-response answers in full, then compare them with the model answers point by point, using the guide [The four free-response questions and how to score](lesson:apstats-frq-strategies).

## Section I: multiple choice

:::quiz test
::mc
The annual incomes in a town are strongly skewed right. Which measure best describes a typical income?
A. The mean
B. The median
C. The range
D. The standard deviation
::key B
The median is resistant to the few very large incomes that pull the mean up.

::mc
What is the interquartile range of the data $4,\ 7,\ 8,\ 10,\ 11,\ 13,\ 30$?
A. $26$
B. $9$
C. $5$
D. $6$
::key D
The median is $10$; $Q_1=7$ (median of $4,7,8$) and $Q_3=13$ (median of $11,13,30$), so $\text{IQR}=6$.

::mc
A data set has $Q_1=20$ and $Q_3=32$. By the $1.5\times\text{IQR}$ rule, which value is an outlier?
A. $52$
B. $45$
C. $49$
D. $14$
::key A
$\text{IQR}=12$, so the fences are $20-18=2$ and $32+18=50$; only $52$ is beyond a fence.

::mc
A test has mean $80$ and standard deviation $4$. What is the z-score of a score of $74$?
A. $1.5$
B. $-6$
C. $-1.5$
D. $-0.67$
::key C
$z=\dfrac{74-80}{4}=-1.5$.

::mc
Two classes have the same mean score, $70$, but class A has standard deviation $5$ and class B has standard deviation $12$. Which statement is correct?
A. Scores in class B are more variable than in class A.
B. Scores in class A are higher than in class B.
C. Class B has more students.
D. Scores in class A are more variable than in class B.
::key A
The standard deviation measures variability; the centres are equal.

::mc
The largest value of a data set is an extreme outlier and is removed. What happens to the mean and the median?
A. Both decrease by the same amount.
B. The median decreases noticeably; the mean changes little.
C. Neither changes.
D. The mean decreases noticeably; the median changes little.
::key D
The mean is pulled by extreme values; the median is resistant.

::mc
A school randomly selects $10$ of its $50$ homerooms and surveys every student in them. This is
A. a stratified random sample
B. a cluster sample
C. a simple random sample of students
D. a systematic sample
::key B
Whole groups are chosen at random and everyone in them is measured.

::mc
A radio show asks listeners to call in and say whether they support a new tax. Which bias is most likely?
A. Nonresponse bias
B. Response bias from wording
C. Voluntary response bias
D. No bias, since the sample is large
::key C
Listeners choose themselves; people with strong opinions are more likely to call.

::mc
Which study design allows a conclusion about cause and effect?
A. A randomized comparative experiment
B. A large observational study
C. A sample survey
D. A census
::key A
Random assignment of treatments makes the groups comparable.

::mc
In an experiment on fertilizers, plots are first grouped by soil type and the fertilizers are then randomly assigned within each group. The main purpose of grouping is to
A. increase the number of treatments
B. allow generalization to all farms
C. remove the need for random assignment
D. reduce variability in the response caused by soil type
::key D
Blocking by soil type removes its effect from the comparison of treatments.

::mc
Which is a statistical investigative question?
A. How many minutes did Minh spend travelling to school today?
B. How many minutes do students at our school typically spend travelling to school?
C. Is travelling to school tiring?
D. At what time does the first lesson start?
::key B
It asks about a group and is answered with data that vary.

::mc
Events $A$ and $B$ are independent with $P(A)=0.5$ and $P(B)=0.3$. What is $P(A\text{ or }B)$?
A. $0.80$
B. $0.15$
C. $0.65$
D. $0.50$
::key C
$P(A\text{ and }B)=0.15$, so $0.5+0.3-0.15=0.65$.

::mc
Of $100$ adults under $30$, $40$ prefer tea; of $200$ adults aged $30$ or over, $80$ prefer tea. A randomly chosen adult from these $300$ prefers tea. What is the probability that the adult is under $30$?
A. $0.333$
B. $0.400$
C. $0.133$
D. $0.500$
::key A
Among the $120$ tea drinkers, $40$ are under $30$: $\dfrac{40}{120}\approx0.333$.

::mc
Questions 14–16 refer to the following. A machine produces parts, $10\%$ of which are defective, independently of each other. Let $X$ be the number of defective parts in a random sample of $8$ parts. What is $P(X=0)$?
A. $0.900$
B. $0.570$
C. $0.430$
D. $0.100$
::key C
$P(X=0)=(0.9)^8\approx0.430$.

::mc
Questions 14–16 refer to the following. A machine produces parts, $10\%$ of which are defective, independently of each other. Let $X$ be the number of defective parts in a random sample of $8$ parts. What is the mean of $X$?
A. $0.1$
B. $0.8$
C. $8$
D. $0.72$
::key B
$\mu_X=np=8(0.1)=0.8$. The value $0.72$ is the variance $np(1-p)$.

::mc
Questions 14–16 refer to the following. A machine produces parts, $10\%$ of which are defective, independently of each other. Let $X$ be the number of defective parts in a random sample of $8$ parts. What is the probability that at least one part is defective?
A. $0.430$
B. $0.800$
C. $0.100$
D. $0.570$
::key D
$P(X\ge1)=1-P(X=0)\approx1-0.430=0.570$.

::mc
A random variable takes the values $0,1,2,3$ with probabilities $0.1,\ 0.3,\ 0.4,\ 0.2$. What is its mean?
A. $1.7$
B. $1.5$
C. $2$
D. $0.25$
::key A
$0(0.1)+1(0.3)+2(0.4)+3(0.2)=1.7$.

::mc
A variable is normally distributed with mean $50$ and standard deviation $8$. What proportion of values are less than $62$?
A. $0.0668$
B. $0.9332$
C. $0.8413$
D. $0.9772$
::key B
$z=\dfrac{62-50}{8}=1.5$ and $P(Z<1.5)\approx0.9332$.

::mc
A population has standard deviation $20$. What is the standard deviation of $\bar x$ for random samples of size $100$?
A. $20$
B. $0.2$
C. $2$
D. $5$
::key C
$\dfrac{20}{\sqrt{100}}=2$.

::mc
In a population, $p=0.04$. What is the smallest of these sample sizes for which the large counts condition holds for $\hat p$?
A. $100$
B. $150$
C. $200$
D. $250$
::key D
We need $np\ge10$: $250(0.04)=10$, while $200(0.04)=8$.

::mc
In a random sample of $400$ voters, $45\%$ support a proposal. Which is a $95\%$ confidence interval for the proportion of all voters who support it?
A. $0.45\pm0.025$
B. $0.45\pm0.098$
C. $0.45\pm0.049$
D. $0.45\pm0.041$
::key C
$1.96\sqrt{\dfrac{0.45(0.55)}{400}}\approx1.96(0.0249)\approx0.049$. The value $0.025$ omits $z^*$, and $0.041$ uses $z^*=1.645$.

::mc
What does "$95\%$ confidence" mean for a confidence interval?
A. About $95\%$ of intervals built this way from many samples capture the true parameter.
B. There is a $95\%$ probability that the sample statistic is in the interval.
C. $95\%$ of the data lie in the interval.
D. The parameter changes $5\%$ of the time.
::key A
The confidence level describes the long-run success rate of the method.

::mc
A company claims that more than $30\%$ of its customers are under $25$. Which hypotheses test the claim?
A. $H_0:p>0.3$, $H_a:p=0.3$
B. $H_0:p=0.3$, $H_a:p>0.3$
C. $H_0:\hat p=0.3$, $H_a:\hat p>0.3$
D. $H_0:p=0.3$, $H_a:p\ne0.3$
::key B
The claim needing evidence goes in $H_a$, and hypotheses are about $p$.

::mc
In a test of $H_0:p=0.5$ with $n=100$, the sample proportion is $0.55$. What is the test statistic?
A. $z=0.10$
B. $z=2.00$
C. $z=0.05$
D. $z=1.00$
::key D
$z=\dfrac{0.55-0.5}{\sqrt{0.5(0.5)/100}}=\dfrac{0.05}{0.05}=1.00$.

::mc
A test gives a P-value of $0.08$ with $\alpha=0.05$. The correct conclusion is
A. fail to reject $H_0$; there is not convincing evidence for $H_a$
B. reject $H_0$; there is convincing evidence for $H_a$
C. accept $H_0$; it is proved true
D. reject $H_a$; $H_0$ is true
::key A
$0.08>0.05$, so we fail to reject $H_0$; this does not prove $H_0$.

::mc
A factory tests $H_0$: the defect rate is $2\%$, against $H_a$: the rate is higher than $2\%$. Which describes a Type II error?
A. Concluding the rate is higher when it really is $2\%$
B. Concluding the rate is $2\%$ when it really is $2\%$
C. Concluding there is not convincing evidence of a higher rate when the rate really is higher
D. Concluding the rate is higher when it really is higher
::key C
A Type II error is failing to reject a false $H_0$.

::mc
What is the smallest sample size for a $95\%$ confidence interval for $p$ with margin of error at most $0.05$, with no prior estimate?
A. $384$
B. $385$
C. $20$
D. $196$
::key B
$n\ge\left(\dfrac{1.96}{0.05}\right)^2(0.25)=384.16$; round up to $385$.

::mc
A $95\%$ confidence interval for $p_1-p_2$ is $(-0.03,\ 0.08)$. Which conclusion is correct?
A. $p_1$ is greater than $p_2$.
B. $p_1$ is less than $p_2$.
C. The two proportions are equal.
D. There is not convincing evidence that the two proportions differ.
::key D
The interval contains $0$, so no difference is plausible; this does not prove they are equal.

::mc
A paired $t$ procedure is used with $12$ pairs. How many degrees of freedom are there?
A. $11$
B. $12$
C. $22$
D. $24$
::key A
Paired data are analysed as one sample of $12$ differences: df $=11$.

::mc
Why is a $t$ interval for a mean wider than a $z$ interval with the same standard error?
A. The sample mean is biased.
B. The $t$ distribution is skewed.
C. Estimating $\sigma$ with $s$ adds uncertainty, so $t^*>z^*$.
D. The sample size is always smaller.
::key C
The $t$ distribution has heavier tails than the normal.

::mc
Which study should be analysed with a paired $t$ procedure?
A. Times of $20$ runners with old shoes are compared with $20$ other runners with new shoes.
B. Each of $20$ runners is timed with old shoes and with new shoes.
C. The proportion of runners who finish is compared for two clubs.
D. The heights of $50$ runners are recorded.
::key B
The same runners are measured twice.

::mc
A random sample of $36$ values has $\bar x=40$ and $s=9$. With $t^*\approx2.030$, the $95\%$ confidence interval for $\mu$ is
A. $(39.49,\ 40.51)$
B. $(21.73,\ 58.27)$
C. $(38.50,\ 41.50)$
D. $(36.95,\ 43.05)$
::key D
The standard error is $\dfrac{9}{6}=1.5$, so $40\pm2.030(1.5)=40\pm3.045$.

::mc
In a test of $H_0:\mu=100$, a sample of $25$ has $\bar x=103$ and $s=10$. What is the test statistic?
A. $t=1.5$
B. $t=0.3$
C. $t=7.5$
D. $t=3.0$
::key A
$t=\dfrac{103-100}{10/\sqrt{25}}=\dfrac{3}{2}=1.5$.

::mc
A random sample of $15$ commute times is strongly skewed right with two outliers. Is a one-sample $t$ interval appropriate?
A. Yes, because the sample is random.
B. Yes, because $15<30$.
C. No, because the sample is small and strongly skewed with outliers.
D. No, because $t$ intervals need $n\ge100$.
::key C
With $n<30$, the normal/large sample condition needs no strong skew or outliers.

::mc
Researchers compare the mean sleep time of a random sample of $40$ boys and an independent random sample of $40$ girls. The appropriate procedure is
A. a two-sample $t$ procedure
B. a paired $t$ procedure
C. a one-sample $z$ test for a proportion
D. a two-sample $z$ test for proportions

Two independent samples and a quantitative variable.

::mc
Questions 36–38 refer to the following. For a group of students, the least-squares line relating weekly practice time $x$ (hours) to quiz score $y$ (points) is $\hat y=3.2+0.85x$, with $r=0.78$. Which is the correct interpretation of $0.85$?

::mc

## Section II: free response

Write your answers in full sentences, with the procedure named, conditions checked and conclusions in context. The points for each part are shown in brackets; each question is worth $10$ points.

:::exercise Question 1 (10 points): Collecting data
A school with $1200$ students, $400$ in each of grades 10, 11 and 12, wants to learn about the time its students spend on homework.

a) Write a statistical investigative question the school could answer with a survey. [2 points]
b) Describe how to select a stratified random sample of $90$ students, using grade as the strata. [4 points]
c) Explain one advantage of stratifying by grade in this situation. [2 points]
d) A teacher suggests instead posting the survey on the school website for anyone to answer. Describe one likely bias and its likely direction. [2 points]

::hint
**What you need:** an investigative question asks about a group and is answered with variable data; a stratified sample takes a simple random sample from each stratum.

::solution
a) A suitable question is: "How many minutes do students at our school typically spend on homework on a school night?" It asks about the whole group of students, and the answers will vary from student to student.

b) Take $30$ students from each grade, since $\dfrac{90}{3}=30$ and the grades have equal sizes. Number the $400$ students in grade 10 from $1$ to $400$, use a random number generator to choose $30$ different numbers from $1$ to $400$ (ignoring repeats), and survey those students. Repeat separately for grade 11 and grade 12.

c) Homework time probably differs between grades, for example older students may have more homework. Stratifying by grade guarantees each grade is represented and gives a more precise estimate of the school's typical homework time than a simple random sample of the same size.

d) Students would choose whether to answer, which is voluntary response bias. Students who spend a lot of time on homework and feel strongly about it are more likely to respond, so the survey would probably overestimate the typical homework time.

So the answers are a clear investigative question, a sample of $30$ students chosen at random within each grade, more precise estimates from stratifying, and a likely overestimate from voluntary response.

**Answer.** See the model answer; key points: $30$ per grade chosen at random, voluntary response bias leading to an overestimate.
:::

:::exercise Question 2 (10 points): Analysing data
Random samples of $40$ customers at each of two cafés give these waiting times, in minutes.

| Café | Min | $Q_1$ | Median | $Q_3$ | Max | Mean | SD |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| A | $2$ | $5$ | $7$ | $9$ | $16$ | $7.4$ | $3.0$ |
| B | $1$ | $3$ | $5$ | $11$ | $22$ | $7.1$ | $5.2$ |

a) Compare the centres and the variability of the two distributions of waiting times. [4 points]
b) Use the $1.5\times\text{IQR}$ rule to decide whether café B has any outliers. [3 points]
c) A customer wants to be fairly sure of not waiting more than $12$ minutes. Which café would you recommend? Justify your choice. [3 points]

::hint
**What you need:** compare with comparative words; for café B, $\text{IQR}=11-3=8$.

::solution
a) Centre: the median waiting time at café B ($5$ minutes) is less than at café A ($7$ minutes), while the means are about the same ($7.1$ and $7.4$ minutes). Variability: waiting times at café B vary much more, with IQR $11-3=8$ minutes compared with $9-5=4$ minutes at café A, and standard deviation $5.2$ compared with $3.0$ minutes.

b) For café B, $\text{IQR}=8$, so the fences are $3-1.5(8)=-9$ and $11+1.5(8)=23$ minutes. The minimum $1$ and the maximum $22$ are both inside the fences, so café B has no outliers.

c) Café A. At café A, $75\%$ of customers waited at most $9$ minutes and the longest wait was $16$ minutes. At café B, about a quarter of customers waited $11$ minutes or more and the longest wait was $22$ minutes, so long waits are more common there, even though the median is lower.

So café B has the shorter typical wait but much more variable waits, has no outliers, and café A is the safer choice for avoiding a wait of more than $12$ minutes.

**Answer.** a) B has a lower median, similar mean, much larger spread; b) no outliers (fences $-9$ and $23$); c) café A.
:::

:::exercise Question 3 (10 points): Inference
Fifty student volunteers were randomly assigned to revise for a test using a new app ($25$ students) or the textbook ($25$ students). The app group's scores had mean $78.4$ and standard deviation $8.1$; the textbook group's scores had mean $73.6$ and standard deviation $9.0$. Dotplots of both groups show no strong skew or outliers.

Do these data give convincing evidence at $\alpha=0.05$ that revising with the app leads to a higher mean score than revising with the textbook? [10 points]

::hint
**What you need:** a two-sample $t$ test; standard error $\sqrt{\dfrac{8.1^2}{25}+\dfrac{9.0^2}{25}}$.

::solution
*State* [2 points]: $H_0:\mu_A-\mu_T=0$ and $H_a:\mu_A-\mu_T>0$, where $\mu_A$ and $\mu_T$ are the true mean scores for students like these who revise with the app and with the textbook; $\alpha=0.05$.

*Plan* [3 points]: two-sample $t$ test for a difference in means. Random: the treatments were randomly assigned. Normal/large sample: each group has $25<30$ students, but the dotplots show no strong skew or outliers.

*Do* [3 points]: the standard error is $\sqrt{\dfrac{8.1^2}{25}+\dfrac{9.0^2}{25}}=\sqrt{2.6244+3.24}\approx2.422$, so
$$t=\frac{78.4-73.6}{2.422}\approx1.98,$$
with df $\approx47.5$ from the calculator, giving a P-value of about $0.027$.

*Conclude* [2 points]: because the P-value of about $0.027$ is less than $\alpha=0.05$, we reject $H_0$. There is convincing evidence that revising with the app leads to a higher mean score than revising with the textbook, for students like these volunteers. Since the treatments were randomly assigned, we can conclude cause and effect, but the volunteers were not a random sample, so we should not generalize to all students.

So the data are convincing at the $5\%$ level.

**Answer.** $t\approx1.98$, P-value $\approx0.027$; reject $H_0$.
:::

:::exercise Question 4 (10 points): Integrated question
A basketball player has made $75\%$ of her free throws over her career. Assume her shots are independent.

a) In a game she takes $12$ free throws. Find the probability that she makes at least $10$ of them. [3 points]
b) This season she made $162$ of $240$ free throws. Treating these shots as a random sample of her current shooting, is there convincing evidence at $\alpha=0.05$ that her current success rate is less than $0.75$? [4 points]
c) Her coach says the drop shows that her new shoes caused her to shoot worse. Explain why the data do not support this conclusion. [2 points]
d) Describe a Type I error in the context of part b). [1 point]

::hint
**What you need:** binomial with $n=12$, $p=0.75$; a one-sample $z$ test with $p_0=0.75$.

::solution
a) The number of successful shots $X$ is binomial with $n=12$ and $p=0.75$, because each shot is a success or failure, shots are independent, $n$ is fixed and $p$ is the same. Then
$$P(X\ge10)=P(X=10)+P(X=11)+P(X=12)\approx0.2323+0.1267+0.0317=0.3907.$$

b) *State:* $H_0:p=0.75$ and $H_a:p<0.75$, where $p$ is her current true success rate; $\alpha=0.05$. *Plan:* one-sample $z$ test; the shots are treated as a random sample; $np_0=180$ and $n(1-p_0)=60$ are both at least $10$. *Do:* $\hat p=\dfrac{162}{240}=0.675$ and $z=\dfrac{0.675-0.75}{\sqrt{0.75(0.25)/240}}=\dfrac{-0.075}{0.02795}\approx-2.68$, so the P-value is about $0.0036$. *Conclude:* because $0.0036<0.05$, we reject $H_0$; there is convincing evidence that her current success rate is less than $0.75$.

c) The shoes were not randomly assigned in an experiment; this is an observational comparison over time. Other variables, such as fatigue, an injury or a change in practice time, could explain the drop, so we cannot conclude that the shoes caused it.

d) A Type I error would be concluding that her success rate has dropped below $0.75$ when in fact it is still $0.75$.

So the probability in a) is about $0.3907$, the evidence in b) is convincing, and the drop cannot be blamed on the shoes.

**Answer.** a) $\approx0.3907$; b) $z\approx-2.68$, P-value $\approx0.0036$, reject $H_0$; c) no random assignment; d) as stated.
:::
