:::goals
- Know the structure of the AP Statistics exam from May 2027: sections, timing, calculators and weighting.
- Know the five units of the revised course, and which topics were removed.
- Recognize the four free-response question types.
- Plan your revision with a ten-week schedule.
:::

AP Statistics is a College Board course equivalent to a one-semester introductory college statistics course. For the 2026–27 school year the course was revised: nine units became five, several topics were removed, and the exam was redesigned. Students from the Vietnamese curriculum usually find the calculations easy; the real challenge is **writing**: explaining methods, checking conditions and stating conclusions in context. These lessons are written for the revised course, so older review books and practice tests will not match it exactly.

## The exam from May 2027

The whole exam is taken in the **Bluebook** app. A graphing calculator is allowed, Desmos is built into Bluebook, and a formula sheet with tables is provided.

| Section | Questions | Time | Weight |
|---|---|---|---|
| I: multiple choice | 42, each with 4 options | 90 minutes | 50% |
| II: free response | 4, each worth 10 points | 90 minutes | 50% |

The multiple-choice section contains **two sets of three questions** that share a stimulus: one set on probability, random variables and distributions, and one on regression. Scores are reported on the AP scale from 1 to 5. Before 2027 the exam had 40 multiple-choice questions with 5 options and 6 free-response questions, including an "investigative task"; that task no longer exists.

## What is tested

| Unit | Content | Share of the multiple-choice section |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Exploring One-Variable Data and Collecting Data | investigative questions, displays and summaries, sampling and experiments | 20%–30% |
| 2. Probability, Random Variables, and Probability Distributions | probability rules, random variables, binomial and normal distributions, sampling distributions | 15%–25% |
| 3. Inference for Categorical Data: Proportions | confidence intervals and tests for proportions | 15%–25% |
| 4. Inference for Quantitative Data: Means | confidence intervals and tests for means | 10%–20% |
| 5. Regression Analysis | scatterplots, correlation, least-squares regression, residuals | 10%–20% |

**Removed from the course:** analysing departures from linearity (transforming data), combining random variables, the geometric distribution, the chi-square goodness-of-fit test, and inference for the slope of a regression line. Skip these in older books. **New:** writing and judging **statistical investigative questions**.

In this section the regression lesson comes right after one-variable data, because both describe data; on the exam regression is Unit 5.

## The four free-response questions

Each question is worth 10 points and has a fixed type:

1. **Formulating questions and collecting data:** investigative questions, sampling methods, experimental design.
2. **Analysing data and interpreting results:** graphs and summary statistics, comparisons, regression output.
3. **Statistical inference:** a confidence interval or a significance test, with conditions and a conclusion in context.
4. **An integrated question** that combines several areas of the course.

## Three sample questions

:::example An investigative question
A student council wants to learn about sleep at their school. Which of the following is a statistical investigative question?
A. How many hours did Lan sleep last night?
B. Is sleep important for students?
C. How many hours do students at our school typically sleep on school nights?
D. What time does the school day start?

**Solution.** A statistical investigative question must be answered with data that **vary**, collected from many individuals, and it asks about a group.

Option A asks about one person, option D has one fixed answer, and option B is an opinion, not a question answered with data. Option C asks about a typical value for a group, and its answer comes from variable data on many students.

So the investigative question is C, answer **C**.
:::

:::example A probability rule
For events $A$ and $B$, $P(A)=0.4$, $P(B)=0.5$ and $P(A\text{ and }B)=0.2$. What is $P(A\text{ or }B)$?
A. $0.9$
B. $0.7$
C. $0.2$
D. $0.5$

**Solution.** By the general addition rule,
$$P(A\text{ or }B)=P(A)+P(B)-P(A\text{ and }B)=0.4+0.5-0.2=0.7.$$

So $P(A\text{ or }B)=0.7$, answer **B**. Option A forgets to subtract the overlap, which would be counted twice.
:::

:::example Interpreting a confidence interval
A random sample of voters gives a $95\%$ confidence interval of $(0.42,\ 0.50)$ for the proportion of all voters in a city who support a new park. Which interpretation is correct?
A. $95\%$ of the voters in the sample support the park.
B. There is a $95\%$ probability that the sample proportion is between $0.42$ and $0.50$.
C. Between $42\%$ and $50\%$ of the voters in the sample support the park.
D. We are $95\%$ confident that the interval from $0.42$ to $0.50$ captures the proportion of all voters in the city who support the park.

**Solution.** A confidence interval estimates a **population** parameter, here the proportion of all voters in the city, and the confidence level describes how reliable the method is.

Options A and C talk about the sample, which we already know exactly, and option B describes the sample proportion, which is the centre of the interval, not something uncertain.

So the correct interpretation is D, answer **D**.
:::

## A ten-week plan

:::note Weeks before the exam
1. **Weeks 1 to 2:** [Describing one-variable data](lesson:apstats-one-variable-data) and [Two-variable data and linear regression](lesson:apstats-two-variable-regression).
2. **Week 3:** sampling, observational studies and experiments.
3. **Weeks 4 to 5:** probability, random variables, normal models and sampling distributions.
4. **Weeks 6 to 8:** inference for proportions, then inference for means; practise writing full tests and intervals.
5. **Weeks 9 to 10:** the free-response types, then a full timed practice exam; review every mistake in an error log.
:::

:::warning Common traps
- **Old materials:** removed topics still appear in older books and past exams; skip them.
- **Answers without context:** "reject $H_0$" alone earns little; say what it means for the situation.
- **Sample versus population:** inference is always about the population parameter.
- **Conditions:** state and check them every time you build an interval or run a test.
:::

AP Classroom and the AP Central course page offer official practice questions, the course and exam description and the formula sheet; use them alongside these lessons.
