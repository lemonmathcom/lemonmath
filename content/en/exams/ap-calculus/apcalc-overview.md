You have met limits, derivatives and integrals in grades 11 and 12, so the content of AP Calculus will not feel new. What more does the exam ask of you? The answer is **justification**: AP wants to see your reasoning explained in words and with theorems, not only a number worked out. It is worth the effort. AP Calculus is one of the most popular Advanced Placement exams, and a good score can earn college credit or placement at many universities in the United States and elsewhere. It also shows admissions officers that you can handle university-level mathematics. We will look at how the exam is built, how AB differs from BC, and how to plan your revision.

## AB or BC?

| | Calculus AB | Calculus BC |
|---|---|---|
| Content | Units 1–8: limits, derivatives, integrals, differential equations and their applications | Everything in AB, plus Units 9–10 (parametric, polar and vector-valued functions; infinite series) and a few extra topics inside Units 6–8 |
| Equivalent to | about one semester of college calculus | about two semesters |
| Scores | one AP score, 1 to 5 | one AP score, plus an AB subscore |

Which one should you choose? If your school or university plan allows it and you are comfortable with the AB content, BC is usually the better choice. The reason is the AB subscore: it shows your performance on the AB part as well, so you still learn how well you did on the AB material.

## The exam format (from May 2027)

In structure, the two exams are the same. The multiple-choice section is taken in the Bluebook app, and you write your free-response answers by hand in a booklet. The table shows how long each part lasts and when a calculator is allowed.

| Section | Part | Questions | Time | Calculator | Weight |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| I. Multiple choice | A | 29 | 62 minutes | not permitted | 50% for Section I |
| | B | 13 | 38 minutes | graphing calculator required | |
| II. Free response | A | 2 | 30 minutes | graphing calculator required | 50% for Section II |
| | B | 4 | 60 minutes | not permitted | |

Multiple-choice questions have four options and there is no penalty for wrong answers, so answer every question, even when you are unsure. Each free-response question is worth 9 points. Older books and websites describe 30 + 15 multiple-choice questions; that format ended with the 2026 exam. The AB exam in 2027 is scheduled for 10 May; check AP Central for the final timetable.

## What free-response questions look like

Free-response questions usually mix several units. Each part is scored separately, so you can earn points on later parts even if an earlier part is wrong. Do not leave a whole question blank because you are stuck on one part. Common types are:

- **Rates in context**, often with a table of values: estimating a derivative, finding a Riemann sum, or working out the total change $\displaystyle\int_a^b r(t)\,dt$.
- **Particle motion**: velocity, acceleration, speeding up or slowing down, total distance.
- **Analysis of a graph of $f'$**: where $f$ increases, has extrema or inflection points, and why.
- **Differential equations**: a slope field, a tangent-line approximation, separation of variables.
- **Area and volume** of regions between curves.
- **BC only**: Taylor polynomials and error bounds, convergence of series, parametric or polar curves.
Each type has its own routine, and a few practice questions make it familiar.

:::warning How free-response answers are marked
Readers look for more than the final number. They look for the **setup** (how you started the problem) and the **justification** (why your conclusion is true). Many students forget this. "The function has a relative maximum at $x=2$" earns little without the reason: "because $f'$ changes from positive to negative at $x=2$". In calculator parts, write the integral or equation you are solving before giving the decimal answer, correct to three decimal places.
:::

## Two sample questions

:::example Multiple choice, no calculator
What is $\displaystyle\lim_{x\to3}\frac{x^2-9}{x-3}$?
A. $0$
B. $3$
C. $6$
D. The limit does not exist.

**Solution.** For $x\ne3$, $\dfrac{x^2-9}{x-3}=x+3$, which approaches $6$. The answer is **C**. Option D is a common error: the function is undefined at $x=3$, but the limit exists.
:::

:::example A free-response part, no calculator
The function $f$ is twice differentiable, and $f'(x)=x^2-4$. On which intervals is $f$ increasing? Justify your answer.

**Solution.** $f'(x)=(x-2)(x+2)>0$ for $x<-2$ and for $x>2$. So $f$ is increasing on $(-\infty, -2]$ and $[2, \infty)$, **because $f'(x)>0$ on these intervals** (except at the endpoints). The justification sentence is what earns the point.
:::

## A study plan

1. **Weeks 1–2:** take a full official practice exam in Bluebook to find your level; review Units 1–3.
2. **Weeks 3–6:** work through Units 4–8 (and 9–10 for BC), one lesson at a time, with the exercises.
3. **Weeks 7–9:** try past free-response questions from AP Central, timed: 15 minutes per question. Compare your work with the official scoring guidelines.
4. **Weeks 10–12:** take full practice exams and go back over your [error log](lesson:error-log).
This plan is only a frame. Stretch or squeeze it to fit the time you have, as long as you keep at it steadily.

Free official resources are AP Classroom (through your school), past free-response questions with scoring guidelines on AP Central, and the practice exams in Bluebook. For calculator skills, see [Using calculators and Desmos wisely](lesson:calculator-skills). If you remember one thing, make it the question we began with: AP wants the reason behind the number. Each time you practise, add one sentence of justification, and keep working steadily.
