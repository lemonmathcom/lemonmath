IB Mathematics: Analysis and Approaches (AA) is the IB Diploma course for students who enjoy algebra, abstraction and proof, and it is the usual choice for future engineers, scientists and economists. It comes at two levels, Standard Level (SL) and Higher Level (HL). This guide describes the current course, whose last exams are in November 2028. A new course will be taught from August 2027 with first exams in May 2029; if you start the Diploma after that, check the official IB pages for the new structure.

## The papers at SL and HL

| | SL | HL |
|---|---|---|
| Paper 1 | 90 minutes, no calculator, 80 marks, 40% | 120 minutes, no calculator, 110 marks, 30% |
| Paper 2 | 90 minutes, graphing calculator (GDC) required, 80 marks, 40% | 120 minutes, GDC required, 110 marks, 30% |
| Paper 3 | none | GDC required, 55 marks, 20%: two extended problem-solving questions |
| Internal assessment | mathematical exploration, 20 marks, 20% | mathematical exploration, 20% |

Paper 3 lasts 1 hour 15 minutes from the 2025 exams onward; older documents still say 1 hour. Papers 1 and 2 each have **Section A** (short questions) and **Section B** (longer, structured questions). A formula booklet is provided in every paper, so the job is not memorizing formulas but knowing when and how to use them. Final grades are on a scale from 1 to 7.

## The five topics

Both levels share the same five topics; HL goes further in each.

1. **Number and algebra:** sequences and series, exponents and logarithms, the binomial theorem, proof. HL adds counting, partial fractions, complex numbers, induction and contradiction.
2. **Functions:** graphs, domain and range, composite and inverse functions, transformations, quadratic, rational, exponential and logarithmic functions.
3. **Geometry and trigonometry:** volume and surface area, the sine and cosine rules, radians, the unit circle, trigonometric identities and equations. HL adds vectors, lines and planes.
4. **Statistics and probability:** data and regression, probability rules, discrete distributions, the binomial and normal distributions. HL adds Bayes' theorem and more on random variables.
5. **Calculus:** limits, differentiation, integration, kinematics and optimization. HL adds Maclaurin series, differential equations and further integration.

## How marks are awarded

IB markschemes split each question into small marks, and knowing the types changes how you write:

- **M marks** reward a correct **method**, even if the arithmetic then goes wrong.
- **A marks** reward an **accurate** answer or intermediate value, usually after the M mark it depends on.
- **R marks** reward clear **reasoning**, such as a justification for rejecting a solution.
- **Follow-through (FT)**: if an earlier part is wrong but you use your wrong value correctly later, you can still earn the later marks.

Two consequences follow. A correct answer with no working may not earn full marks, so always show your method. And a wrong answer with a correct method still earns marks, so never leave a part blank. Unless a question says otherwise, give approximate answers **to three significant figures**, and keep more digits in your calculator until the end.

## Command terms

Every question starts with a command term, and each one asks for something specific. The most important ones:

| Command term | What it asks |
|---|---|
| Write down | Little or no working is needed: the answer can be read off or seen directly. |
| Find / Calculate | Show the main steps of your working. |
| Show that | The answer is given; write every step that leads to it, without starting from the result. |
| Hence | Use the result of the previous part; a different method may earn no marks. |
| Hence or otherwise | Using the previous part is the quickest route, but another valid method is accepted. |
| Sketch | A diagram with the right shape and the key features labeled (intercepts, asymptotes, turning points). |
| Justify / Explain | Give a reason, usually in one clear sentence. |

The mark allocation, written as [n marks] after each part, tells you how much work is expected. A 1-mark part rarely needs more than a line; a 7-mark part needs several steps.

## Paper 1 versus Paper 2

Paper 1 has no calculator, so its questions use exact values: fractions, surds, $\pi$, $\ln2$, exact trigonometric values. Practise algebra and arithmetic by hand until they are fast and reliable.

Paper 2 requires a GDC, and many questions are written so that the calculator does the heavy work: solving equations graphically, finding intersections, evaluating definite integrals, statistics and probability distributions. Learn your calculator's functions well before the exam, and still write the mathematics: state the equation you solved or the integral you evaluated, then the result.

:::tip Use the GDC as a checker on Paper 2
Before moving on, check an answer another way: graph the function to confirm a solution, or substitute it back. A minute of checking protects marks you have already earned.
:::

## A revision plan

:::note Twelve weeks before the exams
1. **Weeks 1 to 6: topic by topic.** For each topic, revise the key ideas, then do mixed past-paper questions on it, Section A first, then Section B. Mark them with the official markschemes.
2. **Weeks 7 to 10: full papers under time.** One Paper 1 and one Paper 2 each week (HL: also Paper 3 every other week). Afterwards, copy each lost mark into an error log, with the reason.
3. **Weeks 11 to 12: weak spots and speed.** Return to the topics your error log shows most often, and practise Section B questions for timing.
:::

:::warning Common traps
- Rounding too early, then giving a final answer that is wrong in the third significant figure.
- Ignoring "hence", then solving a part the long way and losing marks.
- In "show that" questions, starting from the result, or skipping steps because "it is obvious".
- Forgetting radian mode on the GDC for calculus and trigonometric functions.
- Leaving a part blank because an earlier part went wrong: follow-through marks are still available.
:::
