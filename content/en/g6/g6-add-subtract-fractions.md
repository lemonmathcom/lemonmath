In the morning, Mum pours $\dfrac13$ of a litre of orange juice into a jug. At lunchtime she pours in another $\dfrac14$ of a litre. How many litres of orange juice are in the jug now?

Many students would write straight away $\dfrac13+\dfrac14=\dfrac27$. But look again: $\dfrac27$ is even smaller than $\dfrac13$, so pouring in more juice would leave less in the jug. Something is clearly wrong. Adding fractions does not work by "numerator plus numerator, denominator plus denominator". This lesson explains why, and how to do it properly.

## Adding two fractions with the same denominator

When two fractions have the same denominator, the parts are the same size. $\dfrac{2}{7}$ is $2$ parts and $\dfrac{3}{7}$ is $3$ parts, each part being one seventh. Together they make $5$ such parts, that is, $\dfrac{5}{7}$.

:::theorem Adding fractions with the same denominator
To add two fractions with the same denominator, add the numerators and keep the denominator:
$$\dfrac{a}{m}+\dfrac{b}{m}=\dfrac{a+b}{m}.$$
:::

This rule also works when a numerator is negative, because then we are adding two integers. For example, $\dfrac{-5}{9}+\dfrac{2}{9}=\dfrac{-5+2}{9}=\dfrac{-3}{9}=\dfrac{-1}{3}$. The result should be written in its simplest form.

## Adding two fractions with different denominators

Back to the jug of orange juice. A third of a litre and a quarter of a litre are two different kinds of "part", so they cannot be added directly. We change both into the same kind of part, that is, we find a common denominator, just as we did when comparing fractions:
$$\dfrac13+\dfrac14=\dfrac{4}{12}+\dfrac{3}{12}=\dfrac{7}{12}.$$

The jug holds $\dfrac{7}{12}$ of a litre of orange juice, more than either pour. That result makes sense.

:::theorem Adding fractions with different denominators
To add two fractions with different denominators, write them as two fractions with the same positive denominator, then add the numerators and keep the common denominator.
:::

## Properties of addition and opposite numbers

Adding fractions has all the properties of adding integers: it is **commutative**, $\dfrac{a}{b}+\dfrac{c}{d}=\dfrac{c}{d}+\dfrac{a}{b}$, it is **associative**, and **adding $0$** does not change a fraction. So when we add several fractions, we may reorder them and group the ones that "go well" together.

:::definition The opposite of a fraction
Two fractions are called **opposites** if their sum is $0$. The opposite of the fraction $\dfrac{a}{b}$ is written $-\dfrac{a}{b}$, and
$$-\dfrac{a}{b}=\dfrac{-a}{b}=\dfrac{a}{-b}.$$
:::

For example, the opposite of $\dfrac{3}{5}$ is $\dfrac{-3}{5}$, because $\dfrac35+\dfrac{-3}{5}=\dfrac{0}{5}=0$. On the number line, two opposite numbers lie on either side of $0$ at the same distance from $0$, exactly as with integers.

## Subtracting fractions

With integers, we know that subtracting means adding the opposite. Fractions work the same way.

:::theorem Rule for subtracting fractions
To subtract one fraction from another, add to the first fraction the **opposite** of the fraction being subtracted:
$$\dfrac{a}{b}-\dfrac{c}{d}=\dfrac{a}{b}+\left(-\dfrac{c}{d}\right).$$
:::

The bracket rule stays the same: when we remove brackets with a "$-$" sign in front, every term inside changes sign. So everything you learnt about adding and subtracting integers can be used again. The only new thing is the step of finding a common denominator.

:::tip Three steps for adding and subtracting fractions
1. Write the fractions with positive denominators.
2. Find a common denominator (if the denominators differ), using the LCM of the denominators.
3. Add or subtract the numerators as integers, then simplify the result.
:::

## Examples

We start with two fractions that have the same denominator.

:::example Adding fractions with the same denominator
Calculate:

a) $\dfrac{-5}{9}+\dfrac{2}{9}$;

b) $\dfrac{7}{12}+\dfrac{-11}{12}$.

**Solution.**

a) The two fractions have the same denominator, $9$. We add the numerators and keep the denominator:
$$\dfrac{-5}{9}+\dfrac{2}{9}=\dfrac{-5+2}{9}=\dfrac{-3}{9}=\dfrac{-1}{3}.$$

b) In the same way, we have
$$\dfrac{7}{12}+\dfrac{-11}{12}=\dfrac{7+(-11)}{12}=\dfrac{-4}{12}=\dfrac{-1}{3}.$$

So both sums equal $\dfrac{-1}{3}$.
:::

When the denominators differ, the first job is to find a common denominator.

:::example Adding fractions with different denominators
Calculate:

a) $\dfrac{-3}{4}+\dfrac{5}{6}$;

b) $\dfrac{2}{-5}+\dfrac{-3}{10}$.

**Solution.**

a) We have $\text{LCM}(4, 6)=12$. With a common denominator, we add:
$$\dfrac{-3}{4}+\dfrac{5}{6}=\dfrac{-9}{12}+\dfrac{10}{12}=\dfrac{1}{12}.$$

b) First make the denominator positive: $\dfrac{2}{-5}=\dfrac{-2}{5}$. We have $\text{LCM}(5, 10)=10$, so
$$\dfrac{-2}{5}+\dfrac{-3}{10}=\dfrac{-4}{10}+\dfrac{-3}{10}=\dfrac{-7}{10}.$$

So a) $\dfrac{1}{12}$; b) $\dfrac{-7}{10}$.
:::

A subtraction turns into adding the opposite. Write this step out clearly in your first few exercises.

:::example Subtracting fractions
Calculate:

a) $\dfrac{1}{3}-\dfrac{5}{6}$;

b) $\dfrac{-2}{5}-\left(\dfrac{-3}{4}\right)$.

**Solution.**

a) We have
$$\dfrac{1}{3}-\dfrac{5}{6}=\dfrac{1}{3}+\dfrac{-5}{6}=\dfrac{2}{6}+\dfrac{-5}{6}=\dfrac{-3}{6}=\dfrac{-1}{2}.$$

b) The opposite of $\dfrac{-3}{4}$ is $\dfrac{3}{4}$. Hence
$$\dfrac{-2}{5}-\left(\dfrac{-3}{4}\right)=\dfrac{-2}{5}+\dfrac{3}{4}=\dfrac{-8}{20}+\dfrac{15}{20}=\dfrac{7}{20}.$$

So a) $\dfrac{-1}{2}$; b) $\dfrac{7}{20}$.
:::

With several fractions, look for pairs with the same denominator or pairs of opposites before giving everything a common denominator.

:::example Calculating in a sensible order
Calculate:

a) $\dfrac{-5}{7}+\dfrac{3}{8}+\dfrac{5}{7}+\dfrac{-11}{8}$;

b) $\left(\dfrac{2}{3}-\dfrac{5}{11}\right)-\left(\dfrac{-1}{3}+\dfrac{6}{11}\right)$.

**Solution.**

a) Using the commutative and associative properties, we group the fractions with the same denominator:
$$\begin{aligned}
\dfrac{-5}{7}+\dfrac{3}{8}+\dfrac{5}{7}+\dfrac{-11}{8}&=\left(\dfrac{-5}{7}+\dfrac{5}{7}\right)+\left(\dfrac{3}{8}+\dfrac{-11}{8}\right)\\
&=0+\dfrac{-8}{8}\\
&=-1.
\end{aligned}$$

b) Remove the brackets: the first pair keeps its signs; the second pair has a "$-$" sign in front, so its terms change sign:
$$\begin{aligned}
\left(\dfrac{2}{3}-\dfrac{5}{11}\right)-\left(\dfrac{-1}{3}+\dfrac{6}{11}\right)&=\dfrac{2}{3}-\dfrac{5}{11}+\dfrac{1}{3}-\dfrac{6}{11}\\
&=\left(\dfrac{2}{3}+\dfrac{1}{3}\right)-\left(\dfrac{5}{11}+\dfrac{6}{11}\right)\\
&=1-1\\
&=0.
\end{aligned}$$

So a) $-1$; b) $0$.

**Remark.** If we gave all four fractions in part a) the common denominator $56$, we would still get $-1$, but with much more work.
:::

Finally, a spending problem, where fractions help us see the whole picture.

:::example Hà's pocket money
This month Hà spends $\dfrac25$ of her pocket money on books and $\dfrac14$ of it on breakfast. What part of her pocket money does Hà have left?

**Solution.**

*Understand.* Take all of the pocket money as $1$. Hà has spent two amounts, $\dfrac25$ and $\dfrac14$. We need the part that is left.

*Plan.* Subtract the two amounts spent from $1$. The three numbers have denominators $1$; $5$; $4$, so the common denominator is $20$.

*Solve.* The part of the money left is
$$\begin{aligned}
1-\dfrac{2}{5}-\dfrac{1}{4}&=\dfrac{20}{20}-\dfrac{8}{20}-\dfrac{5}{20}\\
&=\dfrac{20-8-5}{20}\\
&=\dfrac{7}{20}.
\end{aligned}$$

So Hà has $\dfrac{7}{20}$ of her pocket money left.

*Look back.* What if Hà's pocket money is $200$ thousand dong? Then $\dfrac{1}{20}$ is $10$ thousand dong, and Hà has $70$ thousand dong left. The fraction gives an answer that is right for any amount; the actual number only needs to go in at the last step.
:::

:::warning Common mistakes
- **Adding numerators together and denominators together.** Wrong: $\dfrac13+\dfrac14=\dfrac27$. Why it is wrong: the result is even smaller than $\dfrac13$, which makes no sense; parts of different sizes cannot be combined directly. Right: $\dfrac13+\dfrac14=\dfrac{4}{12}+\dfrac{3}{12}=\dfrac{7}{12}$.
- **Forgetting to change the sign when subtracting a negative number.** Wrong: $\dfrac{-2}{5}-\left(\dfrac{-3}{4}\right)=\dfrac{-2}{5}-\dfrac{3}{4}$. Why it is wrong: subtracting $\dfrac{-3}{4}$ means adding its opposite, that is, adding $\dfrac34$. Right: $\dfrac{-2}{5}+\dfrac34=\dfrac{7}{20}$.
- **Finding a common denominator but forgetting the numerator.** Wrong: $\dfrac{-3}{4}+\dfrac56=\dfrac{-3}{12}+\dfrac{5}{12}$. Why it is wrong: when the denominator is multiplied by the multiplying factor, the numerator must be multiplied too. Right: $\dfrac{-9}{12}+\dfrac{10}{12}$.
- **Not simplifying the result.** Wrong: leaving the answer as $\dfrac{-4}{12}$. Why it is wrong: answers should be in their simplest form, so they are easy to read and compare. Right: $\dfrac{-4}{12}=\dfrac{-1}{3}$.
:::

## Summary

- Same denominator: $\dfrac{a}{m}+\dfrac{b}{m}=\dfrac{a+b}{m}$.
- Different denominators: give them the same positive denominator, then add the numerators.
- Adding fractions is commutative and associative; adding $0$ changes nothing.
- The opposite of $\dfrac{a}{b}$ is $-\dfrac{a}{b}=\dfrac{-a}{b}$; two opposite numbers add up to $0$.
- Subtracting means adding the opposite: $\dfrac{a}{b}-\dfrac{c}{d}=\dfrac{a}{b}+\left(-\dfrac{c}{d}\right)$; the bracket rule is the same as for integers.

The jug from the opening holds $\dfrac{7}{12}$ of a litre, not $\dfrac27$ of a litre. Every time you finish adding fractions, ask yourself one question: "Is the result bigger than each positive term?" That small question catches a great many mistakes. In the next lesson we learn [multiplying and dividing fractions](lesson:g6-multiply-divide-fractions).

## Practice

Each exercise comes with what you need and how to start. Try it yourself first, then open the solution.

:::exercise
Calculate:

a) $\dfrac{3}{11}+\dfrac{-8}{11}$;

b) $\dfrac{-7}{15}+\dfrac{-4}{15}$;

c) $\dfrac{5}{-9}+\dfrac{2}{9}$.

::hint
**What you need:** $\dfrac{a}{m}+\dfrac{b}{m}=\dfrac{a+b}{m}$.

**Steps:**
1. Make the denominators positive if necessary.
2. Add the numerators, keep the denominator, simplify.

::solution
a) We have $\dfrac{3}{11}+\dfrac{-8}{11}=\dfrac{3+(-8)}{11}=\dfrac{-5}{11}$.

b) We have $\dfrac{-7}{15}+\dfrac{-4}{15}=\dfrac{(-7)+(-4)}{15}=\dfrac{-11}{15}$.

c) We have $\dfrac{5}{-9}=\dfrac{-5}{9}$, so
$$\dfrac{-5}{9}+\dfrac{2}{9}=\dfrac{-5+2}{9}=\dfrac{-3}{9}=\dfrac{-1}{3}.$$

So the results are $\dfrac{-5}{11}$; $\dfrac{-11}{15}$; $\dfrac{-1}{3}$.

**Answer.** a) $\dfrac{-5}{11}$; b) $\dfrac{-11}{15}$; c) $\dfrac{-1}{3}$.
:::

:::exercise
Calculate:

a) $\dfrac{2}{3}+\dfrac{-1}{4}$;

b) $\dfrac{-5}{6}+\dfrac{4}{9}$;

c) $\dfrac{-3}{8}+\dfrac{-5}{12}$.

::hint
**What you need:** with different denominators, use the LCM as the common denominator, then add the numerators.

**Steps:**
1. Find the LCM of the two denominators.
2. Write with the common denominator and add the numerators.

::solution
a) We have $\text{LCM}(3, 4)=12$, so
$$\dfrac{2}{3}+\dfrac{-1}{4}=\dfrac{8}{12}+\dfrac{-3}{12}=\dfrac{5}{12}.$$

b) We have $\text{LCM}(6, 9)=18$, so
$$\dfrac{-5}{6}+\dfrac{4}{9}=\dfrac{-15}{18}+\dfrac{8}{18}=\dfrac{-7}{18}.$$

c) We have $\text{LCM}(8, 12)=24$, so
$$\dfrac{-3}{8}+\dfrac{-5}{12}=\dfrac{-9}{24}+\dfrac{-10}{24}=\dfrac{-19}{24}.$$

So the results are $\dfrac{5}{12}$; $\dfrac{-7}{18}$; $\dfrac{-19}{24}$.

**Answer.** a) $\dfrac{5}{12}$; b) $\dfrac{-7}{18}$; c) $\dfrac{-19}{24}$.
:::

:::exercise
Find the opposite of each number: $\dfrac{4}{7}$; $\dfrac{-5}{9}$; $\dfrac{3}{-8}$; $0$.

::hint
**What you need:** the opposite of $\dfrac{a}{b}$ is $\dfrac{-a}{b}$; two opposite numbers add up to $0$.

**Steps:**
1. Make the denominator positive.
2. Change the sign of the numerator.

::solution
The opposite of $\dfrac{4}{7}$ is $\dfrac{-4}{7}$.

The opposite of $\dfrac{-5}{9}$ is $\dfrac{5}{9}$.

We have $\dfrac{3}{-8}=\dfrac{-3}{8}$, so its opposite is $\dfrac{3}{8}$.

The opposite of $0$ is $0$.

So the opposites are $\dfrac{-4}{7}$; $\dfrac{5}{9}$; $\dfrac{3}{8}$; $0$.

**Answer.** $\dfrac{-4}{7}$; $\dfrac{5}{9}$; $\dfrac{3}{8}$; $0$.
:::

:::exercise
Calculate:

a) $\dfrac{3}{4}-\dfrac{5}{6}$;

b) $\dfrac{-2}{9}-\dfrac{1}{6}$;

c) $\dfrac{1}{2}-\left(\dfrac{-2}{3}\right)$.

::hint
**What you need:** $\dfrac{a}{b}-\dfrac{c}{d}=\dfrac{a}{b}+\left(-\dfrac{c}{d}\right)$.

**Steps:**
1. Turn the subtraction into adding the opposite.
2. Find a common denominator, then add.

::solution
a) We have $\text{LCM}(4, 6)=12$, so
$$\dfrac{3}{4}-\dfrac{5}{6}=\dfrac{9}{12}+\dfrac{-10}{12}=\dfrac{-1}{12}.$$

b) We have $\text{LCM}(9, 6)=18$, so
$$\dfrac{-2}{9}-\dfrac{1}{6}=\dfrac{-4}{18}+\dfrac{-3}{18}=\dfrac{-7}{18}.$$

c) The opposite of $\dfrac{-2}{3}$ is $\dfrac23$, so
$$\dfrac{1}{2}-\left(\dfrac{-2}{3}\right)=\dfrac{1}{2}+\dfrac{2}{3}=\dfrac{3}{6}+\dfrac{4}{6}=\dfrac{7}{6}.$$

So the results are $\dfrac{-1}{12}$; $\dfrac{-7}{18}$; $\dfrac{7}{6}$.

**Answer.** a) $\dfrac{-1}{12}$; b) $\dfrac{-7}{18}$; c) $\dfrac{7}{6}$.
:::

:::exercise
Calculate in a sensible order:

a) $\dfrac{-3}{7}+\dfrac{5}{13}+\dfrac{-4}{7}$;

b) $\left(\dfrac{7}{10}-\dfrac{3}{8}\right)-\left(\dfrac{7}{10}-\dfrac{5}{8}\right)$.

::hint
**What you need:** the commutative and associative properties; the bracket rule.

**Steps:**
1. Part a): group the two fractions with denominator $7$.
2. Part b): remove the brackets, then group opposite numbers.

::solution
a) We group the two fractions with denominator $7$:
$$\begin{aligned}
\dfrac{-3}{7}+\dfrac{5}{13}+\dfrac{-4}{7}&=\left(\dfrac{-3}{7}+\dfrac{-4}{7}\right)+\dfrac{5}{13}\\
&=-1+\dfrac{5}{13}\\
&=\dfrac{-13}{13}+\dfrac{5}{13}=\dfrac{-8}{13}.
\end{aligned}$$

b) The second pair of brackets has a "$-$" sign in front, so the signs change when we remove it:
$$\begin{aligned}
\left(\dfrac{7}{10}-\dfrac{3}{8}\right)-\left(\dfrac{7}{10}-\dfrac{5}{8}\right)&=\dfrac{7}{10}-\dfrac{3}{8}-\dfrac{7}{10}+\dfrac{5}{8}\\
&=\left(\dfrac{7}{10}-\dfrac{7}{10}\right)+\left(\dfrac{5}{8}-\dfrac{3}{8}\right)\\
&=0+\dfrac{2}{8}=\dfrac{1}{4}.
\end{aligned}$$

So a) $\dfrac{-8}{13}$; b) $\dfrac{1}{4}$.

**Answer.** a) $\dfrac{-8}{13}$; b) $\dfrac{1}{4}$.
:::

:::exercise
Find $x$, given that:

a) $x+\dfrac{1}{4}=\dfrac{-2}{3}$;

b) $\dfrac{3}{5}-x=\dfrac{-1}{10}$.

::hint
**What you need:** an unknown term equals the sum minus the known term; the number subtracted equals the first number minus the difference.

**Steps:**
1. Write the calculation that gives $x$.
2. Find a common denominator and calculate.

::solution
a) We have $x+\dfrac{1}{4}=\dfrac{-2}{3}$, so
$$x=\dfrac{-2}{3}-\dfrac{1}{4}=\dfrac{-8}{12}+\dfrac{-3}{12}=\dfrac{-11}{12}.$$

b) We have $\dfrac{3}{5}-x=\dfrac{-1}{10}$. The number subtracted equals the first number minus the difference, so
$$x=\dfrac{3}{5}-\left(\dfrac{-1}{10}\right)=\dfrac{6}{10}+\dfrac{1}{10}=\dfrac{7}{10}.$$

Check: $\dfrac{3}{5}-\dfrac{7}{10}=\dfrac{6}{10}-\dfrac{7}{10}=\dfrac{-1}{10}$, which is correct.

So a) $x=\dfrac{-11}{12}$; b) $x=\dfrac{7}{10}$.

**Answer.** a) $x=\dfrac{-11}{12}$; b) $x=\dfrac{7}{10}$.
:::

:::exercise
A family uses water from a tank. On the first day they use $\dfrac{1}{3}$ of the tank, and on the second day $\dfrac{2}{5}$ of the tank. After the two days, what part of the tank have they used, and what part is left?

::hint
**What you need:** take the whole tank as $1$; add the two parts used, then subtract from $1$.

**Steps:**
1. Work out $\dfrac13+\dfrac25$.
2. Subtract the result from $1$.

::solution
The part of the water used in the two days is
$$\dfrac{1}{3}+\dfrac{2}{5}=\dfrac{5}{15}+\dfrac{6}{15}=\dfrac{11}{15}\ (\text{of the tank}).$$

The part of the water left in the tank is
$$1-\dfrac{11}{15}=\dfrac{15}{15}-\dfrac{11}{15}=\dfrac{4}{15}\ (\text{of the tank}).$$

So after the two days the family has used $\dfrac{11}{15}$ of the tank, and $\dfrac{4}{15}$ of the tank is left.

**Answer.** Used $\dfrac{11}{15}$ of the tank; $\dfrac{4}{15}$ left.
:::

:::exercise
A piece of cloth is $5\dfrac{1}{2}$ m long. First $1\dfrac{3}{4}$ m is cut off, then $2\dfrac{1}{3}$ m. How many metres of cloth are left?

::hint
**What you need:** change the mixed numbers into fractions; the common denominator of $2$; $4$; $3$ is $12$.

**Steps:**
1. Change the three mixed numbers into fractions.
2. Subtract the two pieces cut off from the starting length.

::solution
Change the mixed numbers into fractions:
$$5\dfrac{1}{2}=\dfrac{11}{2},\qquad 1\dfrac{3}{4}=\dfrac{7}{4},\qquad 2\dfrac{1}{3}=\dfrac{7}{3}.$$

The length of cloth left is
$$\begin{aligned}
\dfrac{11}{2}-\dfrac{7}{4}-\dfrac{7}{3}&=\dfrac{66}{12}-\dfrac{21}{12}-\dfrac{28}{12}\\
&=\dfrac{66-21-28}{12}\\
&=\dfrac{17}{12}=1\dfrac{5}{12}\ (\text{m}).
\end{aligned}$$

So $1\dfrac{5}{12}$ m of cloth is left.

**Answer.** $1\dfrac{5}{12}$ m.
:::

:::exercise Challenge
Work out the sum
$$S=\dfrac{1}{1\cdot2}+\dfrac{1}{2\cdot3}+\dfrac{1}{3\cdot4}+\dots+\dfrac{1}{9\cdot10}.$$

::hint
**What you need:** for a natural number $n\ge1$, we have $\dfrac{1}{n}-\dfrac{1}{n+1}=\dfrac{1}{n(n+1)}$.

**Steps:**
1. Check this equality for $n=1$ and $n=2$.
2. Write each term as a difference, then see which numbers cancel out.

::solution
For a natural number $n\ge1$, with a common denominator we have
$$\dfrac{1}{n}-\dfrac{1}{n+1}=\dfrac{n+1}{n(n+1)}-\dfrac{n}{n(n+1)}=\dfrac{1}{n(n+1)}.$$

Applying this to each term of $S$:
$$\begin{aligned}
S&=\left(\dfrac{1}{1}-\dfrac{1}{2}\right)+\left(\dfrac{1}{2}-\dfrac{1}{3}\right)+\left(\dfrac{1}{3}-\dfrac{1}{4}\right)+\dots+\left(\dfrac{1}{9}-\dfrac{1}{10}\right).
\end{aligned}$$

Removing the brackets, the pairs of opposites $-\dfrac12$ and $\dfrac12$, $-\dfrac13$ and $\dfrac13$, …, $-\dfrac19$ and $\dfrac19$ add up to $0$. Only the first and the last terms remain:
$$S=1-\dfrac{1}{10}=\dfrac{9}{10}.$$

So $S=\dfrac{9}{10}$.

**Answer.** $S=\dfrac{9}{10}$.
:::
