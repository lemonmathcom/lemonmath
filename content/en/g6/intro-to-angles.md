- Tell acute, right, obtuse and straight angles apart; work out the angle between the hands of a clock at simple times.
:::

Look at a clock at $3$ o'clock: the two hands make a familiar shape, like the corner of a page. At $6$ o'clock the hands stretch out into a straight line. At $1$ o'clock the hands are close together, like a narrow pair of scissors. All three shapes are **angles**; they differ only in how wide they "open".

The question of this lesson: how can we measure that opening with a number, so we can say exactly how much bigger one angle is than another? The answer is the size of an angle, measured in degrees.

## Angles

:::definition Angle
An **angle** is a figure made of two rays with the same endpoint. The common endpoint is called the **vertex** of the angle; the two rays are called the **arms** of the angle.
:::

The angle in the picture has vertex $O$ and arms ray $Ox$ and ray $Oy$. We call it angle $xOy$ (or angle $yOx$, or angle $O$), written $\widehat{xOy}$. When an angle is named with three letters, **the middle letter is always the vertex**. If points $A$, $B$ lie on the two arms, the angle can also be written $\widehat{AOB}$.

When the arms $Ox$, $Oy$ are not opposite rays, a point $M$ like the one in the picture is called a **point inside** angle $xOy$: it lies "between" the two arms, within the opening of the angle.

## The size of an angle

To measure an angle, we use a **protractor** (usually a half circle divided into $180$ equal parts, each part being $1$ **degree**, written $1^\circ$).

:::tip How to measure angle $xOy$
1. Place the protractor so that its centre is exactly on the vertex $O$.
2. Turn the protractor so that one arm, say $Ox$, passes through the $0$ mark.
3. Read the number where arm $Oy$ crosses the **same scale** as that $0$ mark: that number is the size of the angle.
:::

A protractor usually has two scales (an inner and an outer scale, running in opposite directions). You must read the scale whose $0$ is on the first arm. A useful check: a narrow angle must measure less than $90^\circ$; if you read $115^\circ$ for a narrow angle, you have certainly read the wrong scale.

The size of angle $xOy$ is written, for example, $\widehat{xOy}=60^\circ$. Every angle has a size, and the size of an angle is at most $180^\circ$. Two angles are **equal** if they have the same size; the angle with the larger size is the larger angle.

## Kinds of angle

By their sizes, angles are sorted into four kinds.

:::definition Acute, right, obtuse and straight angles
- An angle of $90^\circ$ is a **right angle**.
- An angle greater than $0^\circ$ and less than $90^\circ$ is an **acute angle**.
- An angle greater than $90^\circ$ and less than $180^\circ$ is an **obtuse angle**.
- An angle of $180^\circ$ is a **straight angle**: its arms are opposite rays.
:::

The right angle is the "landmark" for comparing: an acute angle is smaller than a right angle, an obtuse angle is bigger than a right angle but smaller than a straight angle. A set square has a right angle, so we use it to check quickly whether an angle is a right angle.

## Examples

We start with naming.

:::example Naming an angle
Angle $mOn$ is given.

a) State the vertex and the arms of the angle.

b) Write the angle in symbols in two ways.

**Solution.**

a) The middle letter is the vertex, so the vertex of the angle is $O$. The two arms are ray $Om$ and ray $On$.

b) Angle $mOn$ can be written $\widehat{mOn}$ or $\widehat{nOm}$.

So the angle has vertex $O$ and arms $Om$, $On$; it is written $\widehat{mOn}$ or $\widehat{nOm}$.
:::

How many angles are there in a figure made of several rays with the same endpoint?

:::example Counting angles
Draw three different rays $Ox$, $Oy$, $Oz$ with the same endpoint $O$. How many angles have vertex $O$ and two of these rays as arms? Name them.

**Solution.**

An angle is fixed by its two arms. From three rays we can choose three pairs: $Ox$ and $Oy$; $Oy$ and $Oz$; $Ox$ and $Oz$.

So there are $3$ angles: $\widehat{xOy}$, $\widehat{yOz}$, $\widehat{xOz}$.
:::

Sorting angles just needs a comparison of the size with $90^\circ$ and $180^\circ$.

:::example Sorting angles
What kind of angle has each of these sizes: $35^\circ$; $90^\circ$; $127^\circ$; $180^\circ$; $89^\circ$?

**Solution.**

We compare each size with $90^\circ$ and $180^\circ$:

$0^\circ<35^\circ<90^\circ$, so an angle of $35^\circ$ is acute.

An angle of $90^\circ$ is a right angle.

$90^\circ<127^\circ<180^\circ$, so an angle of $127^\circ$ is obtuse.

An angle of $180^\circ$ is a straight angle.

$0^\circ<89^\circ<90^\circ$, so an angle of $89^\circ$ is acute.

So the angles of $35^\circ$ and $89^\circ$ are acute; $90^\circ$ is a right angle; $127^\circ$ is obtuse; $180^\circ$ is a straight angle.

**Remark.** An angle of $89^\circ$ looks very like a right angle, but it is still acute. In geometry, "almost equal" is not "equal".
:::

Reading a protractor needs care in choosing the scale.

:::example Reading a protractor
An measures angle $xOy$: the centre of the protractor is on $O$, and arm $Ox$ passes through the $0$ mark of the inner scale. Arm $Oy$ crosses the mark labelled $65$ on the inner scale and $115$ on the outer scale. What is the size of angle $xOy$? What kind of angle is it?

**Solution.**

Arm $Ox$ passes through the $0$ of the inner scale, so we read the size on the inner scale. Arm $Oy$ crosses $65$ on the inner scale, so
$$\widehat{xOy}=65^\circ.$$

Since $0^\circ<65^\circ<90^\circ$, $\widehat{xOy}$ is an acute angle.

So $\widehat{xOy}=65^\circ$, and it is an acute angle.

**Remark.** If we read the outer scale by mistake, we get $115^\circ$, an obtuse angle. Looking at the picture to see whether the angle is acute or obtuse catches this mistake at once.
:::

Finally, angles on a clock face.

:::example The angle between the hands of a clock
Find the angle between the hour hand and the minute hand at exactly $2$ o'clock, $3$ o'clock and $4$ o'clock. What kind of angle is each one?

**Solution.**

*Understand.* At an exact hour the minute hand points to $12$ and the hour hand points exactly to the hour. We need the angle between the hands.

*Plan.* A full turn is $360^\circ$ (two straight angles). The clock face is split into $12$ equal gaps, so each gap between two neighbouring numbers is $360^\circ:12=30^\circ$. We count the gaps between the hands and multiply by $30^\circ$.

*Solve.* At $2$ o'clock there are $2$ gaps between the hands, so the angle is $2\cdot30^\circ=60^\circ$, an acute angle.

At $3$ o'clock there are $3$ gaps, so the angle is $3\cdot30^\circ=90^\circ$, a right angle.

At $4$ o'clock there are $4$ gaps, so the angle is $4\cdot30^\circ=120^\circ$, an obtuse angle.

So at $2$, $3$ and $4$ o'clock the angle between the hands is $60^\circ$ (acute), $90^\circ$ (right) and $120^\circ$ (obtuse).

*Look back.* At $6$ o'clock there are $6$ gaps, giving $180^\circ$: the hands are in a straight line, a straight angle, just as we saw in the opening.
:::

:::warning Common mistakes
- **Not putting the vertex in the middle.** Wrong: the angle with vertex $O$ and arms $Ox$, $Oy$ is written $\widehat{Oxy}$. Why it is wrong: in a three-letter name, the middle letter is the vertex. Right: $\widehat{xOy}$.
- **Reading the wrong scale on the protractor.** Wrong: reading $115^\circ$ for an acute angle. Why it is wrong: you must read the scale whose $0$ is on the first arm. Right: read the same scale, giving $65^\circ$.
- **Thinking an angle close to $90^\circ$ is a right angle.** Wrong: an angle of $89^\circ$ is a right angle. Why it is wrong: a right angle is exactly $90^\circ$. Right: $89^\circ$ is an acute angle.
- **Thinking longer arms make a bigger angle.** Wrong: drawing longer arms makes the angle larger. Why it is wrong: the arms are rays that go on for ever; the size depends only on the opening between them. Right: the angle is the same whether its arms are drawn long or short.
:::

## Summary

- An angle is made of two rays with the same endpoint; the endpoint is the vertex, the rays are the arms. Angle $xOy$ is written $\widehat{xOy}$, with the vertex in the middle.
- Measure an angle with a protractor: centre on the vertex, one arm through $0$, read the same scale at the other arm. The unit is the degree.
- Acute: less than $90^\circ$; right: exactly $90^\circ$; obtuse: more than $90^\circ$ and less than $180^\circ$; straight: exactly $180^\circ$.
- On a clock face, the gap between two neighbouring numbers is $30^\circ$.

The clock hands from the opening now have sizes: $90^\circ$ at $3$ o'clock, $180^\circ$ at $6$ o'clock, $30^\circ$ at $1$ o'clock. From now on, each time you check the time, try working out what angle the hands make. In the next lesson we move to a new topic: [collecting and showing data](lesson:data-collection). If you need to revise rays, see [Points and lines](lesson:points-and-lines).

## Practice

Each exercise comes with what you need and how to start. Try it yourself first, then open the solution.

:::exercise
Angle $\widehat{ABC}$ is given. State its vertex and its arms.

::hint
**What you need:** the middle letter is the vertex; the arms are the two rays starting at the vertex.

**Steps:**
1. Find the middle letter.
2. Write the two rays from the vertex through the other two points.

::solution
In $\widehat{ABC}$ the middle letter is $B$, so the vertex of the angle is $B$.

The arms of the angle are ray $BA$ and ray $BC$.

So the angle has vertex $B$ and arms $BA$ and $BC$.

**Answer.** Vertex $B$; arms $BA$, $BC$.
:::

:::exercise
Draw four different rays $Ox$, $Oy$, $Oz$, $Ot$ with the same endpoint $O$. How many angles have vertex $O$ and two of these rays as arms? Name them.

::hint
**What you need:** each pair of rays with the same endpoint forms one angle.

**Steps:**
1. List the pairs of rays without repeats.
2. Count.

::solution
Each pair of rays forms one angle. We list them without repeats:

With arm $Ox$: $\widehat{xOy}$, $\widehat{xOz}$, $\widehat{xOt}$.

With arm $Oy$ (leaving out the angle already listed): $\widehat{yOz}$, $\widehat{yOt}$.

With arm $Oz$ (leaving out those already listed): $\widehat{zOt}$.

So there are $3+2+1=6$ angles.

**Answer.** $6$ angles: $\widehat{xOy}$, $\widehat{xOz}$, $\widehat{xOt}$, $\widehat{yOz}$, $\widehat{yOt}$, $\widehat{zOt}$.
:::

:::exercise
What kind of angle has each of these sizes: $60^\circ$; $150^\circ$; $90^\circ$; $179^\circ$; $15^\circ$; $180^\circ$?

::hint
**What you need:** compare the size with $90^\circ$ and $180^\circ$.

**Steps:**
1. Less than $90^\circ$: acute; exactly $90^\circ$: right.
2. Between $90^\circ$ and $180^\circ$: obtuse; exactly $180^\circ$: straight.

::solution
The angles of $60^\circ$ and $15^\circ$ are less than $90^\circ$, so they are acute.

The angle of $90^\circ$ is a right angle.

The angles of $150^\circ$ and $179^\circ$ are more than $90^\circ$ and less than $180^\circ$, so they are obtuse.

The angle of $180^\circ$ is a straight angle.

So acute: $60^\circ$, $15^\circ$; right: $90^\circ$; obtuse: $150^\circ$, $179^\circ$; straight: $180^\circ$.

**Answer.** Acute: $60^\circ$, $15^\circ$; right: $90^\circ$; obtuse: $150^\circ$, $179^\circ$; straight: $180^\circ$.
:::

:::exercise
Find the angle between the hour hand and the minute hand at exactly $1$ o'clock, $5$ o'clock and $6$ o'clock. What kind of angle is each one?

::hint
**What you need:** the gap between two neighbouring numbers on a clock face is $30^\circ$.

**Steps:**
1. Count the gaps between the hands.
2. Multiply by $30^\circ$.

::solution
At $1$ o'clock there is $1$ gap between the hands, so the angle is $30^\circ$, an acute angle.

At $5$ o'clock there are $5$ gaps, so the angle is $5\cdot30^\circ=150^\circ$, an obtuse angle.

At $6$ o'clock there are $6$ gaps, so the angle is $6\cdot30^\circ=180^\circ$, a straight angle.

So the angles are $30^\circ$ (acute), $150^\circ$ (obtuse) and $180^\circ$ (straight).

**Answer.** $30^\circ$; $150^\circ$; $180^\circ$.
:::

:::exercise
Is each angle of these shapes acute, right or obtuse: a square, an equilateral triangle, a regular hexagon?

::hint
**What you need:** each angle of a square is $90^\circ$; of an equilateral triangle $60^\circ$; of a regular hexagon $120^\circ$.

**Steps:**
1. Recall the size of each angle of each shape.
2. Compare with $90^\circ$.

::solution
Each angle of a square is $90^\circ$, so it is a right angle.

Each angle of an equilateral triangle is $60^\circ<90^\circ$, so it is acute.

Each angle of a regular hexagon is $120^\circ$, more than $90^\circ$ and less than $180^\circ$, so it is obtuse.

So square: right angles; equilateral triangle: acute angles; regular hexagon: obtuse angles.

**Answer.** Right; acute; obtuse.
:::

:::exercise
Bình measures angle $mOn$: the centre of the protractor is on $O$, and arm $On$ passes through the $0$ mark of the outer scale. Arm $Om$ crosses the mark labelled $140$ on the outer scale and $40$ on the inner scale. The picture shows that angle $mOn$ is obtuse. Find the size of angle $mOn$.

::hint
**What you need:** read the scale whose $0$ is on the first arm.

**Steps:**
1. Decide which scale to read.
2. Check against the shape of the angle.

::solution
Arm $On$ passes through the $0$ of the outer scale, so we read the size on the outer scale. Arm $Om$ crosses $140$ on the outer scale, so
$$\widehat{mOn}=140^\circ.$$

Check: $90^\circ<140^\circ<180^\circ$, so it is indeed obtuse, as in the picture.

So $\widehat{mOn}=140^\circ$.

**Answer.** $140^\circ$.
:::

:::exercise
In one hour the minute hand turns exactly one full turn, that is, $360^\circ$. Through how many degrees does the minute hand turn in $15$ minutes? In $20$ minutes?

::hint
**What you need:** each minute the minute hand turns $360^\circ:60=6^\circ$.

**Steps:**
1. Find the turn in $1$ minute.
2. Multiply by the number of minutes.

::solution
In $60$ minutes the minute hand turns $360^\circ$. So in $1$ minute it turns
$$360^\circ:60=6^\circ.$$

In $15$ minutes it turns $15\cdot6^\circ=90^\circ$.

In $20$ minutes it turns $20\cdot6^\circ=120^\circ$.

So the minute hand turns $90^\circ$ in $15$ minutes and $120^\circ$ in $20$ minutes.

**Answer.** $90^\circ$; $120^\circ$.
:::

:::exercise
Triangle $ABC$ has $\widehat{A}=50^\circ$, $\widehat{B}=60^\circ$, $\widehat{C}=70^\circ$. Name the three angles of the triangle (using three letters) and say what kind each one is.

::hint
**What you need:** the angle at vertex $A$ of triangle $ABC$ is $\widehat{BAC}$.

**Steps:**
1. Write each angle's name with the vertex in the middle.
2. Compare each size with $90^\circ$.

::solution
The three angles of triangle $ABC$ are $\widehat{BAC}$ (vertex $A$), $\widehat{ABC}$ (vertex $B$) and $\widehat{ACB}$ (vertex $C$).

We have $\widehat{BAC}=50^\circ$, $\widehat{ABC}=60^\circ$, $\widehat{ACB}=70^\circ$. All three sizes are less than $90^\circ$, so all three angles are acute.

So the three angles are $\widehat{BAC}$, $\widehat{ABC}$, $\widehat{ACB}$, and all are acute.

**Answer.** $\widehat{BAC}$, $\widehat{ABC}$, $\widehat{ACB}$; all three are acute.
:::

:::exercise Challenge
Find the angle between the hour hand and the minute hand at half past $3$ ($3$:$30$).

::hint
**What you need:** in $60$ minutes the hour hand moves one gap, that is, $30^\circ$; in $30$ minutes it moves half a gap.

**Steps:**
1. Find where the minute hand points at half past $3$.
2. How many degrees has the hour hand moved past the $3$? Work out the angle between the hands.

::solution
At half past $3$, the minute hand points exactly to $6$.

In $60$ minutes the hour hand moves one gap, that is, $30^\circ$. Thirty minutes after $3$ o'clock, the hour hand has moved past the $3$ by
$$30^\circ:2=15^\circ,$$
and lies between the $3$ and the $4$.

From $3$ to $6$ there are $3$ gaps, that is, $3\cdot30^\circ=90^\circ$. The hour hand has moved $15^\circ$ towards the $6$, so the angle between the hands is
$$90^\circ-15^\circ=75^\circ.$$

So at half past $3$ the angle between the hands is $75^\circ$.

**Answer.** $75^\circ$.
:::
